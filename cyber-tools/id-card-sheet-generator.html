    What is an ID card maker?

    An ID card maker is a tool that turns a list of people into printable identity cards. Instead of drawing a card by hand in a design program and then copying it once per person, you describe the card once — its size, its layout, which fields it shows, what colours and logo it carries — and then feed in the people. The tool repeats the design for every person and fills in their own name, number and photograph.

    That distinction matters more than it sounds. A school secretary making 340 student cards in a word processor spends most of the day nudging text boxes. The same 340 cards from a spreadsheet, through a generator, is a paste, a check of the preview, and a print job. The design work happens once.

    This particular ID card maker adds a second job on top: the sheet generator. Real ID cards are small. A standard card is 85.6 × 54 mm, which means eight of them fit on an A4 page with comfortable 10 mm margins, and ten if you tighten the margins and gaps. Printing them one to a page would waste most of your card stock, so the tool arranges as many cards as will fit onto each sheet, adds crop marks so you know where to cut, and paginates the rest.

    The whole flow in one line: data → ID cards → A4 sheet → print. Everything on this page is in service of moving you along that line quickly and without surprises at the printer.

    How to create an ID card online

    The tool is arranged as six steps. On a wide screen they are all visible at once as a three-pane editor; on a phone you move through them one at a time.

    1. Choose a template and card size. Eight templates cover the common cases. Pick the one closest to what you want — you can change every colour afterwards, so treat it as a starting layout rather than a final commitment. The default size is CR80, the credit-card size.
    2. Add a person and their photo. Type a name, an ID number and whichever other details you need. Drop in a photograph and crop it so the face sits in the frame. On a phone you can shoot the photo directly.
    3. Add your branding. Organisation name, tagline, card title, footer line, contact line and logo are shared by every card, so you set them once. This is also where you switch individual fields on and off.
    4. Check the card preview. The preview is drawn at the exact card proportions, so what you see is what the printer produces. Step through people with the previous and next buttons to spot a name that has run long or a photo that needs re-cropping.
    5. Arrange the sheet. Choose the paper, margins and spacing. The tool reports the grid it worked out — for example 2 × 4, 8 cards per page — and lays the cards out.
    6. Print or download. Print straight from the browser, or export a PDF or PNG to print later or send to a print shop.

    How to make multiple ID cards on one A4 sheet

    Fitting several cards on a page is arithmetic, and the tool does it for you, but it helps to know the shape of the calculation because it explains why a small change to a margin suddenly costs you a whole row.

    The usable width of the page is the paper width minus both margins. Into that you fit as many card widths as possible, remembering that every card after the first also needs a horizontal gap in front of it. The same applies vertically. In other words:

    • columns = whole number of times (card width + gap) fits into (usable width + gap)
    • rows = the same calculation with heights
    • cards per page = columns × rows

    On A4 portrait — 210 × 297 mm — with 10 mm margins and 4 mm gaps, a landscape CR80 card gives you 2 columns and 4 rows, so 8 cards per page. Two columns is the hard ceiling across the width, because a third card would need 273 mm and there are only 190 mm to play with. The rows are where the give is: drop the margin to 5 mm and the gaps to 2 mm and a fifth row appears, taking you to 10 cards per page. Switch the cards to portrait instead and the grid becomes 3 × 3, which is 9 per page — one more card, in a shape that hangs better on a lanyard.

    If you need more per sheet, the levers in order of usefulness are: reduce the gaps to 2 mm, reduce the margin to 5 mm if your printer can reach that close to the edge, and try the paper in landscape, which sometimes reshuffles the grid in your favour — A4 landscape takes CR80 cards 3 × 3, for instance. The statistics panel updates instantly, so you can try each one and watch the count.

    Employee ID cards

    An employee card has to do two jobs: identify the person to a human at a glance, and carry whatever machine-readable or administrative identifier your organisation runs on. That usually means a photograph large enough to recognise from a metre away, a name in a size that reads across a reception desk, and an employee number that is unambiguous when typed into a system.

    The employee and office templates are built around that hierarchy — name largest, designation and department secondary, employee number in a fixed position at the bottom where a security guard knows to look. A valid-until date is worth adding even for permanent staff, because it forces cards out of circulation on a schedule instead of leaving old badges working forever.

    A practical detail: put the return address or a contact number in the footer line. Lost badges get handed in far more often when there is somewhere obvious to hand them in to.

    Student ID cards

    Student cards carry more fields than staff cards, and the fields are different. Class and section, roll number, date of birth and an emergency contact number all tend to be required, and the address often is too, particularly where the card doubles as a bus pass or a library card.

    That is a lot of text on 85.6 × 54 mm. Two things help. First, switch off any field you do not actually need — every field you remove gives the rest more room and makes the card easier to read. Second, use the portrait orientation for student cards. A portrait card stacks details vertically under the photograph, which suits a long list of short values much better than a landscape card does, and it hangs correctly on a lanyard without twisting.

    School ID cards

    School cards are student cards with the institution pushed to the front. The school name, crest and often a house or campus name go in the header band, in the school's own colours. Set those colours once in the design panel and every card on every sheet picks them up.

    Schools also tend to print in batches by class. The bulk import is the fastest route: export one class from your management system as CSV, paste it, check the mapping, and generate. Then repeat for the next class with the same design settings still in place. If different classes need different colours, change the header colour between batches — the setting is one click and applies to the whole sheet.

    Office ID cards

    Office and corporate cards usually mean something more restrained: a narrow accent panel rather than a broad coloured band, a smaller logo, a lot of white space. The office template uses a vertical accent strip with the photograph inside it, which reads as more formal than a header band and leaves the rest of the card clean.

    If your organisation has a brand guideline, the four colour controls — header, background, text and accent — are usually enough to match it exactly. Paste your brand hex value into the accent field and the card, the strip and the dividing rules all follow it.

    Event and visitor ID cards

    Event badges and visitor passes have the opposite priority to staff cards: the name matters and almost nothing else does. People need to read each other's names across a room, so the name should be as large as the card allows, with the company or role beneath it in a much smaller size.

    The event template does exactly that and adds a coloured band at the foot for the event name. The visitor template puts a large VISITOR word mark at the top — deliberately loud, because the entire point of a visitor pass is that it is distinguishable from a staff badge from across a lobby — and gives the valid-until date real prominence so a day pass is obviously a day pass.

    For events, the larger 105 × 74 mm badge size is worth considering. It holds a much bigger name and fits standard badge holders. It is a greedy size on A4 portrait — only 3 fit, in a single column — so switch the paper to A4 landscape, where you get 4 per sheet in a 2 × 2 grid.

    How to print ID cards correctly

    Nearly every complaint about ID card printing comes down to one thing: the browser or printer driver scaled the page and nobody noticed. A card that should be 85.6 mm comes out at 82 mm, which is invisible to the eye but fatal if the card has to fit a rigid holder.

    The fix is to check three settings in the print dialog every time:

    • Scale: 100% or Actual size. Never Fit to page or Shrink to fit. Those options exist to pull content inside the printer's unprintable border, and they do it by shrinking everything by a few percent.
    • Headers and footers: off. Otherwise the browser adds the page URL and date, which can push the layout and waste a strip of paper.
    • Background graphics: on. Some browsers strip background colours by default, which would print your coloured header bands as white.

    If your printer genuinely cannot print within 10 mm of the paper edge, do not solve it by scaling. Increase the page margin in the tool instead — that keeps the cards at their true size and simply moves them inwards.

    Finally, do a single test page on plain paper before committing card stock. Measure one printed card with a ruler. If it measures 85–86 mm you are set; if it measures 82 mm, something is still scaling.

    Paper and finishing

    Ordinary 80 gsm paper is fine for testing and for disposable event badges. For cards that will be handled daily, use 220–300 gsm card stock, or print on paper and laminate afterwards. If you laminate, leave 3–4 mm of clear film around each card so the seal holds — that means cutting slightly outside the crop marks rather than exactly on them.

    Portrait vs landscape ID cards

    Landscape is the default for a reason: it matches wallets, card readers and the way most people expect an identity card to look. Choose landscape when the card will be carried in a pocket, swiped in a reader, or when the design leans on a horizontal photo-left, details-right layout.

    Portrait suits lanyards. A landscape card on a lanyard rotates and ends up sideways; a portrait card hangs square. Portrait also handles long lists of short fields better, because each one gets its own line rather than competing for width. Schools, conferences and hospitals tend to use portrait for that reason.

    One thing to keep in mind: switching orientation changes how many cards fit on a page, and not always in the direction you expect. Check the statistics panel after you switch.

    How to create bulk ID cards

    Bulk mode exists because typing 200 names is not a reasonable use of anyone's afternoon. There are two ways in.

    Paste from a spreadsheet. Select the rows in Excel, Google Sheets or LibreOffice — including the header row — copy, and paste into the data box. Spreadsheets copy as tab-separated text, which the parser recognises automatically. This is the fastest route and needs no file at all.

    Load a CSV or TSV file. Choose the file and it is read locally into the same data box. Quoted fields containing commas are handled correctly, so an address like "21 Ring Road, Pune" stays in one column.

    Either way, the next screen shows you two things before anything is created: the column mapping it guessed, and a preview table of the actual rows. Correct any mapping that is wrong, check that the preview looks like your data rather than like a mangled version of it, and only then create the cards. Rows with no name are flagged rather than silently imported.

    Photographs cannot travel in a CSV. After importing, select each person in the list and attach their photograph — or leave the photo frames empty, which is perfectly normal for temporary passes and library cards.

    How to prepare data for bulk ID cards

    Ten minutes tidying a spreadsheet saves an hour of fixing cards afterwards. What matters:

    • One header row, then one person per row. No merged cells, no blank spacer rows, no title row above the headers.
    • Name the headers recognisably. Name, Full Name, ID, Employee ID, Roll No, Class, Department, Designation, Phone, Email, DOB and Valid Until are all matched automatically. Anything else you map by hand in one click.
    • Format dates the way you want them printed. The tool prints date text exactly as it receives it, so decide on 12/2027 or 31-12-2027 in the spreadsheet, not afterwards.
    • Watch the length of the longest value. Names and designations are shrunk to fit the card automatically, but a 45-character designation will end up small. Shorten it in the data if you can.
    • Check for duplicate ID numbers before importing. A duplicate badge number causes real problems later and is trivially avoidable now.

    How to print ID cards on A4 paper

    Step by step, from a finished sheet preview:

    1. Confirm the statistics panel shows the grid and page count you expect.
    2. Turn on crop marks. They cost nothing and make cutting far more accurate than eyeballing the card edges.
    3. Press Print ID Cards. Only the sheet pages go to the printer — the editor, the site header and this article are all excluded.
    4. In the print dialog set scale to 100%, headers and footers off, background graphics on.
    5. Print one page on plain paper first and measure a card.
    6. Load your card stock and print the rest.
    7. Cut with a guillotine or trimmer against the crop marks. A trimmer gives a far cleaner edge than scissors, and stacking two or three sheets at a time is faster and stays square.

    If you would rather not print from the browser at all — for instance because the job is going to a print shop — download the PDF instead. It is built at the exact page size you selected, so the shop does not have to guess or rescale.

    ID card design tips

    • Make the photo bigger than feels necessary. Recognition at a distance is the card's main job. A photo that looks generous on screen usually looks about right in a lanyard at arm's length.
    • One strong colour, not four. A single accent used for the header band, the divider rule and the number label looks designed. Three competing colours looks like a template.
    • Keep contrast high. Dark text on a light background stays legible under bad lighting and survives a cheap printer. Light text on a mid-tone band is where legibility goes to die.
    • Leave a quiet margin. Nothing important should sit within 3 mm of the card edge — trimming is never perfectly accurate, and a name clipped by a millimetre looks careless.
    • Be consistent about names. Decide on Priya Sharma or SHARMA, PRIYA and apply it to every row before importing.
    • Use headshots, not group-photo crops. Face centred, shoulders visible, plain background, even light. The cropper will help, but it cannot invent detail that a distant snapshot never had.
    • Print a proof of the worst case. Find the longest name and the darkest photo in your batch and print those two first.

    Privacy and browser-based processing

    ID card data is personal data. Names, photographs, dates of birth, addresses and employee numbers together are exactly the kind of set that ought not to be scattered across random web services.

    This tool avoids the problem by not having a server side. Photographs are read with the browser's FileReader API, decoded into an <img>, and drawn onto a <canvas>. The cards, the sheet preview, the PNG export and the PDF are all produced from that canvas by JavaScript running in your tab. There is no endpoint to upload to, so there is nothing to delete afterwards.

    Two honest caveats, because vague security claims help nobody:

    • Your draft — settings, text fields and photographs — is saved in this browser's localStorage so a refresh does not lose your work. That is data at rest on your own machine. On a shared or public computer, press Reset everything when you finish, which clears it.
    • Printing hands the document to your operating system's print system, and downloading writes a file to your disk. What happens to a spooled print job or a saved PDF after that is governed by your own machine and network, not by this page.

    Beyond that, the page loads no third-party code into the card-building path and sends no card data anywhere.

    Frequently asked questions

    Everything people usually want to know before making a batch of cards.

    How do I make an ID card online?

    Choose a template, type in the name and ID number, upload a photograph and crop it, then add your organisation name and logo. The card preview updates as you type. When you are happy with it, add everyone else who needs a card and move on to the sheet layout, which arranges the cards on printable paper for you.

    Is the ID card maker free?

    Yes. Every template, the bulk import, the sheet layout and both the PDF and PNG exports are free, with no account, no watermark and no per-card limit.

    Can I create multiple ID cards at once?

    Yes. Add people one at a time with the Add Person button, or paste a table from Excel or Google Sheets and let the tool map the columns. Each person gets their own card, and you can also set how many copies of each card to print — useful when someone needs a spare.

    Can I print ID cards on A4 paper?

    Yes, that is what the sheet generator is for. It positions the cards on A4 using real millimetre measurements, so a card that says 85.6 × 54 mm comes out of the printer at that size — as long as you print at 100% scale rather than Fit to page.

    How many ID cards fit on an A4 sheet?

    With standard CR80 cards at 85.6 × 54 mm, an A4 portrait page with 10 mm margins and 4 mm gaps holds 8 cards — two per row across four rows. Tighten the margin to 5 mm and the gaps to 2 mm and a fifth row fits, giving 10. Portrait cards fit 9 per page in a 3 × 3 grid, and A3 takes 18. The tool recalculates the exact count whenever you change the card size, paper, margins or spacing, and shows it in the statistics panel.

    Can I upload photos for each person?

    Yes. Drag a photo in, pick one from your device, or use your phone camera. JPG, PNG and WebP all work. Large photos are resized in the browser before use so the page stays fast, and the built-in cropper lets you zoom and pan the face into the frame.

    Can I create employee ID cards?

    Yes. The employee and office templates carry a name, designation, department, employee number, organisation name and logo, plus an optional valid-until date and a footer line for a return address.

    Can I create student and school ID cards?

    Yes. The school and student templates include class and section, roll number, date of birth, an address line and an emergency phone number, which is the set of fields most schools ask for. Switch off anything you do not need and the remaining fields get more room.

    Can I upload a CSV file of names?

    Yes. Upload a CSV or TSV file, or paste rows straight from a spreadsheet. Column headers such as Name, ID, Department or Roll No are matched to card fields automatically, and you can correct any mapping before the cards are created. Quoted values containing commas are parsed correctly.

    Can I customise the ID card design?

    Yes. Header, background, text and accent colours, six font families, text size, photo shape, rounded or square card corners, an optional signature line, and which fields appear at all are all adjustable. The template only decides where things start.

    Can I add my company logo?

    Yes. Upload a PNG, JPG, WebP or SVG logo and it appears in the card header. The image is read in your browser and is never sent anywhere. Its aspect ratio is preserved so it is not stretched.

    Can I change the ID card size?

    Yes. The default is CR80, the 85.6 × 54 mm size used by credit cards and most access badges. There are presets for CR79, a large lanyard card and an event badge, and you can enter any custom width and height in millimetres.

    Can I make portrait and landscape ID cards?

    Yes. Each template has a natural orientation but you can switch at any time. The card layout and the arrangement on the sheet both adjust, and the cards-per-page figure updates so you can see the trade-off.

    Can I download the ID cards as a PDF?

    Yes. The PDF is built inside your browser at the exact page size you chose, with each card placed at its measured position and crop marks drawn as thin vector lines. You can also export the sheet as a PNG, or download a single card on its own.

    Does the ID card maker work on a phone?

    Yes. On a small screen the tool becomes a step-by-step flow — template, people, design, card preview, sheet, print — with large touch targets and a sticky next button. You can take a photo with the phone camera and use it straight away.

    Is my personal information uploaded to a server?

    No. Photographs, names, ID numbers and the finished cards are processed by JavaScript running on your device. There is no upload step in this tool at all, so nothing is transmitted to ToolAdda. Your draft is saved in this browser's local storage so a refresh does not lose your work; Reset everything clears it.

    Can I create ID cards without signing up?

    Yes. There is no account, no email address and no trial. Open the page and start building cards.

    Why do my printed cards come out slightly small?

    Almost always because the print dialog is set to Fit to page or Shrink to fit, which scales the sheet down by a few percent to clear the printer's unprintable border. Set the scale to 100% or Actual size, and turn off headers and footers. If your printer cannot print close to the paper edge, increase the page margin in the tool rather than scaling — that keeps the cards at true size and just moves them inwards.

    Can I add a QR code or barcode to the card?

    Not inside this tool yet. In the meantime, generate the codes with the QR Code Generator or the Barcode Generator, print them on the reverse of the sheet, or add them to the card artwork before printing.
