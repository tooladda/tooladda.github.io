Why the ruling has to match the letters

Four-line ruling is how English handwriting is taught: a top line, a dashed middle line, a solid writing line, and a bottom line. Small letters — a, c, e, o — sit between the dashed line and the writing line. Tall letters — h, l, b, d — reach the top line. Letters with tails — g, p, y, q — drop below the writing line to the bottom one.

Those relative heights are most of what handwriting practice is teaching. A sheet whose letters do not match its own lines teaches the wrong thing, which is why this generator works the other way round from most: it takes the chosen font's real proportions and calculates where the lines go, instead of fixing the lines and scaling the letters to roughly fit. At any line height, an ascender ends exactly on the top line and a small letter ends exactly on the dashed one.

Which tracing style to use

Faded letters

The whole letter shape in light grey. Easiest for the youngest children — the pencil simply covers what is already there. Also the most reliable on a home inkjet.

Dotted outline

The classic school style. A clearer path to follow than a solid fade, and it stops the child from simply colouring in the shape.

Hollow outline

Only the edges. For children who already form letters and are working on size and consistency rather than shape.

Choosing a line height

A rough guide: 18–22 mm for nursery and kindergarten, 12–16 mm for early primary, and 8–11 mm once handwriting is established. Start larger than seems necessary. A child who cannot fit a letter into the space available learns to write cramped, and that is much harder to unlearn than writing too big.

The rest of the controls

Start dots

A dot marking where each item begins, for children who know the letter shape but not where to put the pencil down. It marks the starting point only — genuine stroke-order arrows would need path data for every glyph in every font, and a tool that guessed at them would teach the wrong stroke.

Guide colour

Grey, blue or very light. Blue is what most printed workbooks use, because a child's pencil shows up against it more clearly than against grey. Very light suits a confident writer who only needs a hint. The model row stays black whichever you pick — it is the reference, not a guide.

Repeat or wrap

Repeat fills each row with the same item, which is what letter drills need. Wrap as a sentence flows a whole sentence across the rows instead, for a child copying a line rather than practising a shape. Words are measured, so a line breaks where it actually runs out of room.

Letter case

Switch the whole sheet to UPPERCASE, lowercase or Capitalised without retyping. Useful when the same word list is needed in capitals one week and small letters the next.

Copy link

Copy link puts the entire sheet — text, ruling, size, every setting — into the URL. Send it to a parent or a colleague and they open the same worksheet rather than a screenshot and a list of instructions. Nothing is stored on a server; the settings travel in the link itself.

Practise letters in families, not in alphabet order

A–Z is the order letters are named in, not the order they are learned in. Handwriting is taught in families — groups that start with the same pencil movement — because a child who has mastered the anticlockwise curve of c already has most of a, d, g, o and q. Drilling the alphabet in order asks them to switch movement on almost every letter.

The four lines, in plain words

Baseline

The solid line letters sit on. Every letter starts from it or returns to it, which is why it is drawn darker than the others on these sheets.

x-height

The band between the dashed middle line and the baseline — the height of a small x. Letters like a, c, e, o and s stop here. Getting a consistent x-height is most of what makes handwriting look neat.

Ascender

The part of b, d, f, h, k, l and t that reaches up to the top line. A child whose ascenders only reach the dashed line is writing every tall letter as a short one.

Descender

The tail of g, j, p, q and y that drops below the baseline to the bottom line. Descenders are usually the last thing to settle.

Left-handed writers

Two things help more than any worksheet setting. Let the paper tilt clockwise, the opposite way to a right-hander, so the hand sits below the writing line rather than dragging across it. And ask for the pencil to be held a little further from the tip, which stops the hand covering what has just been written.

On the sheet itself, the model row, then trace layout is worth choosing: a left-hander's hand covers a model placed on the same line, but a model on the row above stays visible while they write.

A rough order to work through

  1. Patterns before letters. Loops, waves and zig-zags across a blank or single-line sheet build the movement without the pressure of a shape that has to be right. For plain ruled, graph or dot-grid paper to practise on, the paper generator prints it.
  2. Capitals. Straight-line capitals first — they are the most forgiving, since a wobbly straight line still reads as the letter.
  3. Small letters by family, using the groups above rather than A to Z.
  4. Their own name. The most motivating word there is, and the one-tap Name practice sheet is set up for it.
  5. Common words. The sight-words sheet, with a blank row after each so they copy rather than only trace. For number and sum practice alongside the writing, the worksheet generator covers maths.
  6. Sentences. Switch Row content to wrap, drop the line height, and the practice becomes about spacing and rhythm rather than letter shapes.

Printing

Press Print and choose Save as PDF if you want a file. Two settings matter in the print dialog: set the scale to 100% rather than "Fit to page", and turn off headers and footers. The sheet is laid out in real millimetres, so at 100% the line height you chose is the line height that comes out of the printer. Rows are set never to split across a page break.

Hindi and other Indian languages

Devanagari is not written the way English is. Its letters hang from a headline — the shirorekha — rather than sitting on a baseline, and matras drop below. Four-line Latin ruling is simply the wrong paper for it.

So there is a Devanagari ruling: a strong line at the top for the letters to hang from and a light one below for the matras, with the letters sized to the band between them. Choosing the हिंदी स्वर preset switches to it automatically.

One honest limit remains. The five handwriting faces here are Latin fonts, so Devanagari falls back to your system font — it prints correctly and lines up with the ruling, but it will not look handwritten the way the English letters do. If what you want is typed text that looks handwritten rather than a sheet to write on, that is the text to handwriting tool instead.

Frequently asked questions

What is four-line ruling and why does it matter?

A top line, a dashed middle line, a writing line and a bottom line. Small letters sit in the middle band, tall letters reach the top, and tailed letters drop to the bottom. Those relative heights are most of what the practice is teaching.

Do the letters actually line up with the ruling?

Yes, by construction. The lines are calculated from the font's own proportions, so an ascender ends exactly on the top line at every line height — rather than the usual approach of fixing the lines and scaling the font to roughly fit.

Which tracing style should I choose?

Faded for the youngest, dotted for the classic school look, outline for children already forming letters. All three print with no watermark.

Can I make a name tracing worksheet?

Yes — there is a one-tap Name practice sheet. Tap it and replace the sample name with the child's own; it already comes with big rows, a dotted outline and a model row on top.

Does it support Hindi or other Indian languages?

Yes — there is a Devanagari ruling with a headline for the letters to hang from, and the Hindi preset switches to it automatically. One limit remains: the handwriting faces are Latin fonts, so Devanagari falls back to your system font and will not look handwritten.

How do I get a PDF instead of printing?

Press Print and choose "Save as PDF". The text stays vector, so it is crisp at any zoom.

Why does the printed sheet look different from the screen?

Almost always the browser scaling to fit. Set the print scale to 100% and turn off headers and footers.

What line height should I use?

18–22 mm for nursery, 12–16 mm for early primary, 8–11 mm once handwriting is established. Start larger than you think.

Is anything I type uploaded?

No. The sheet is built and printed by your browser. Only the handwriting font is fetched externally, from Google Fonts.

What order should a child learn letters in?

By family rather than A to Z. Letters that start with the same pencil movement are taught together — the anticlockwise curve of c carries most of a, d, g, o and q — so a child is not switching movement on every letter. The tables above list the families and load them into the sheet in one tap.

Should we start with capitals or small letters?

Capitals, and the straight-line ones first — E, F, H, I, L, T. They are the most forgiving shapes, because a slightly wobbly straight line still reads as the right letter, so a child gets an early win before tackling curves.

Can this make cursive or joined-up writing sheets?

Not properly. The fonts here are print handwriting faces, so letters are formed correctly but not joined. A true cursive sheet needs a font with connecting entry and exit strokes; using a script font without them produces letters that look joined in the preview and are not, which teaches the wrong join.

Any advice for a left-handed child?

Tilt the paper clockwise rather than anticlockwise so the hand sits below the line instead of dragging through it, and hold the pencil slightly further from the tip so the hand does not cover what was just written. On the sheet, pick the model row then trace layout — a model on the row above stays visible, one on the same line does not.

How many practice sheets a day is reasonable?

Short and often beats long and occasional. Five to ten minutes daily does more than a full page once a week, and a page of the same letter past the point of concentration mostly practises the mistakes. That is what rows per item is for — two or three good rows, not twenty.

Why do the letters look different from my child's school book?

Schools pick a house style and stick to it, and they differ — some teach a looped f, some a straight one; some teach an entry stroke on small letters, some do not. Five fonts are offered here so you can pick the closest match. If none of them matches, follow the school's book for shapes and use these sheets for size and spacing practice.

Can I use these sheets for an adult improving their handwriting?

Yes. Drop the line height to 8–10 mm, choose the hollow outline so only the shape is given, and use the sentence wrap so the practice is about spacing and consistency rather than letter formation, which an adult already knows.

How is this different from the Text to Handwriting tool?

Opposite jobs. That one turns typed text into something that looks handwritten. This one makes a blank practice sheet for someone to write on.
