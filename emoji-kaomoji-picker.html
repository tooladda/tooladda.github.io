Everything to know about emoji, kaomoji, and text symbols

What is an emoji picker?

An emoji picker is a search-and-select tool that lets you find and copy an emoji without hunting through your phone's tiny built-in keyboard grid. Instead of scrolling through dozens of unlabelled icons, you type what you mean — "laugh", "fire", "birthday" — and get a shortlist you can copy with a single tap. A good picker also goes further than your phone's keyboard: it surfaces kaomoji, text symbols, and decorative characters that most keyboards don't offer at all, and it works the same way on a laptop, where native emoji access is often buried behind an obscure shortcut.

What is kaomoji? (Japanese emoticons explained)

Kaomoji (顔文字) is the Japanese word for "face character." Unlike a Western emoticon such as :), which you read sideways, a kaomoji is read face-on: (＾▽＾) looks like a face with the eyes, mouth, and outline all upright. Kaomoji emerged on Japanese bulletin boards in the early 1990s, arguably before Western emoticons became widespread on IRC and Usenet, and they've since spread globally because they express nuance plain emoji can't — a specific kind of shy blush, an exact shrug, a very particular flavour of rage typically expressed by flipping a table.

How to use kaomoji on WhatsApp, Instagram, Discord, and X

Copy the kaomoji here, then paste it directly into the message box, caption, bio, or username field with a long-press → Paste (mobile) or Ctrl/Cmd + V (desktop). Discord and X handle the widest range of Unicode symbol blocks; some older or heavily-filtered input fields (certain banking or government forms) strip anything outside basic Latin text, so if a kaomoji "disappears" on paste, that's the field's filter, not a copy failure on this page.

History and origin of emoji and kaomoji

Kaomoji is generally traced to a 1986 Japanese message board post using (^_^). Emoji followed in 1999, created by Shigetaka Kurita for Japanese mobile carrier NTT DoCoMo as a 12×12 pixel set of 176 icons meant to make short messages friendlier. Apple's iOS emoji keyboard (2008, expanded globally in 2011) and the founding of the Unicode Consortium's emoji working group turned emoji into a global, standardized character set rather than a Japan-only feature — which is also why every emoji now has an official Unicode codepoint, the same way the letter "A" does.

Emoji categories explained

The Unicode emoji standard groups every emoji into official categories — Smileys & Emotion, People & Body, Animals & Nature, Food & Drink, Travel & Places, Activities, Objects, Symbols, and Flags — and this picker follows the same structure so the layout matches what you'd expect from a phone keyboard, just with better search on top.

Emoji meanings people get wrong

  • 🙏 is "folded hands" (please/thanks/pray), not a high-five.
  • 😂 means tears of joy from laughing, not tears of sadness.
  • 🍑 is officially "peach," though it's widely used with an unofficial slang meaning — context matters.
  • 💯 is "hundred points," used for "exactly right" or "keep it 100," not literally a school grade.
  • 😬 is "grimacing face" (awkward/cringe), often mistaken for a grin.

Emoji for social media marketing and engagement

On Instagram and X, one or two well-placed emoji at the start of a caption line measurably improve scannability and can lift engagement, but overuse reads as spam and can trigger platform-level reach penalties. A practical rule: use emoji to replace a word (🔥 instead of "amazing"), not to decorate every sentence. If you're drafting captions with a strict character limit, run the draft through our character counter before posting — emoji count as more than one character on some platforms, which quietly eats into your limit.

Emoji in messaging, email, and professional communication

In Slack and WhatsApp, emoji are expected shorthand. In professional email, a single closing emoji (🙂) can soften tone, but stacking several reads as unprofessional in most industries. Kaomoji are almost never appropriate in formal email, since many corporate mail filters and older webmail clients don't render the Unicode ranges cleanly.

Why some emoji look different on iPhone, Android, and Windows

Unicode standardizes the meaning and codepoint of an emoji, not its artwork. Apple, Google, Samsung, and Microsoft each design and ship their own emoji font, so 😳 can look flushed-and-wide-eyed on one platform and subtly different on another, while still representing the same underlying character and the same intended meaning.

Unicode, shortcodes, and how emoji actually work

Every emoji is one or more Unicode codepoints — for example 😀 is U+1F600. Apps like Slack and Discord also support human-readable shortcodes such as :grinning:, which their software maps back to the real character. Tap the ⓘ detail view on any tile in this picker to see the exact codepoint, HTML entity, CSS escape, and shortcode for that item — genuinely useful if you're a developer hardcoding an icon into markup instead of pasting it as a live character.

Skin tones, gender variants, and ZWJ sequences

Since Unicode 8.0, people-emoji support five Fitzpatrick skin tone modifiers layered onto a base emoji (for example 👋 + 🏽 → 👋🏽). More complex emoji, like a family or a specific-gender professional, are built as a ZWJ sequence — several codepoints joined invisibly with a Zero-Width Joiner so multiple characters render as one combined glyph. Older devices that don't recognise a ZWJ sequence will show the separate pieces side by side instead of the merged icon.
