Complete guide

What is BMI and how is it calculated?

Related tools: Age Calculator · Percentage Calculator · Scientific Calculator · All tools

What is BMI (Body Mass Index)?

Body Mass Index is a simple screening number calculated from your height and weight that estimates whether your weight falls into a range associated with higher or lower health risk at a population level. It was developed in the 1830s by Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet and later adopted by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) as a standardized, low-cost screening tool. BMI does not measure body fat directly, diagnose disease, or replace a clinical assessment — it is a starting point that flags who might benefit from a closer look.

The BMI formula: metric and imperial

The metric BMI formula is straightforward: BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in metres)². The imperial version uses a conversion constant to keep the same output scale: BMI = 703 × weight in pounds ÷ (height in inches)². Both formulas describe the exact same physical relationship — mass relative to height squared — they simply start from different measurement units.

How BMI is calculated: a worked example

Take someone who is 170 cm tall and weighs 70 kg. Convert height to metres (1.70 m), square it (1.70 × 1.70 = 2.89), then divide weight by that figure: 70 ÷ 2.89 = 24.2. That places them in the WHO "Normal weight" band. In imperial units, the same person is roughly 5 ft 7 in (67 inches) and 154 lb: 703 × 154 ÷ (67 × 67) = 108,362 ÷ 4,489 ≈ 24.1 — matching the metric result within rounding. This calculator performs the conversion automatically regardless of which unit system you choose.

BMI categories explained

The WHO's global adult BMI categories are used by most public health bodies, including the CDC:

These bands apply to adults aged 18 and over, independent of age or sex. Children and teens are instead assessed using age- and sex-specific BMI percentile charts, since healthy body fat naturally shifts throughout growth.

Healthy weight range explained

Your "healthy weight range" is simply the span of body weights that would keep your BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 at your current height — the boundary of the WHO normal-weight band. This calculator computes both ends of that range for you (or 18.5–22.9 if you enable Asia-Pacific cutoffs) and shows exactly how far your current weight is from either edge.

Ideal weight: Devine, Robinson, Miller, and Hamwi formulas

Beyond the BMI-derived healthy range, several older but still widely referenced clinical formulas estimate a single "ideal body weight" from height and sex — originally developed for medication dosing calculations. The Devine formula (1974) is the most commonly cited in clinical pharmacy; Robinson (1983) and Miller (1983) refined it with adjusted coefficients; and the Hamwi method (1964) is a simpler rule of thumb still taught in nutrition courses. Because each formula gives a slightly different number, this calculator averages all four and shows the spread, which is more representative than trusting any single formula in isolation.

BMI for men vs women

The BMI formula and its category cutoffs are identical regardless of sex — there is no separate "male BMI" or "female BMI" scale. What does differ is body composition at a given BMI: women, on average, carry a higher percentage of essential body fat than men at the same BMI, which is why sex-specific formulas are used elsewhere in this dashboard for ideal weight, BMR, and body fat percentage, even though the BMI number itself is calculated the same way for everyone.

BMI for older adults

Muscle mass tends to decline with age (a process called sarcopenia) while fat mass often stays the same or increases, meaning an older adult can have a "normal" BMI while carrying a higher percentage of body fat than a younger adult with the same BMI — sometimes called sarcopenic obesity. Because of this, waist circumference and functional strength measures are increasingly recommended alongside BMI for adults over roughly 65, and some geriatric guidelines suggest a slightly higher healthy BMI ceiling in very old age is not automatically concerning.

BMI for athletes and highly muscular people

Muscle tissue is denser than fat tissue, so a very muscular person can weigh enough to register as "overweight" or even "obese" by BMI while having low body fat and excellent cardiometabolic health. This is a well-documented limitation, not an edge case — it affects bodybuilders, strength athletes, and many active people with above-average muscle mass. Enabling Athlete Mode in this calculator surfaces that caveat directly next to your result, and the waist-to-height ratio and body fat estimate below the gauge are more informative in this situation than the BMI category alone.

BMI during pregnancy

Standard BMI categories are not designed for pregnancy, since healthy, expected weight gain during gestation would otherwise be misclassified as "overweight" or "obese" by the normal scale. Pregnancy weight gain recommendations instead depend on pre-pregnancy BMI and are set by an obstetric care provider — the U.S. Institute of Medicine, for instance, publishes trimester-specific weight-gain ranges based on starting BMI category. Enabling Pregnancy Mode in this tool disables the standard category interpretation and displays a reminder to follow your own doctor's guidance rather than a general-purpose calculator.

Limitations of BMI

BMI is a screening ratio, not a body-composition measurement — it cannot tell muscle from fat, cannot see where fat is distributed (a major factor in cardiometabolic risk), and was originally derived from and validated on European population data, which affects its accuracy across some ethnic groups. It can misclassify athletes as overweight, under-flag "normal weight obesity" (a normal BMI with high body fat and low muscle), and does not apply to children, pregnant women, or, in the same way, to older adults. Used correctly, BMI is a fast first-pass screening signal that should be interpreted alongside waist measurements, body composition where available, and a clinician's assessment — not a standalone diagnosis.

Waist circumference and cardiometabolic risk

Because BMI cannot see fat distribution, waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) have become popular complementary measures, since abdominal (visceral) fat carries a stronger, more direct link to cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes risk than total body weight. A commonly cited, easy-to-remember guideline is to keep your waist circumference below half your height — a WHtR under 0.5. This calculator computes WHtR automatically whenever you enter a waist measurement in Advanced Options.

Waist-to-hip ratio explained

Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) compares abdominal circumference to hip circumference to describe body fat distribution shape — commonly summarized as "apple" (fat concentrated around the waist) versus "pear" (fat concentrated around the hips and thighs). The World Health Organization classifies a WHR above 0.90 in men or above 0.85 in women as indicating substantially increased health risk, independent of overall BMI.

Body fat percentage vs BMI

Body fat percentage measures what fraction of your total body mass is fat tissue — a more direct picture of body composition than BMI's simple weight-and-height ratio. Gold-standard measurement methods like DEXA scans and hydrostatic ("underwater") weighing require clinical equipment, but the U.S. Navy circumference method — used in this calculator — provides a reasonably accurate tape-measure estimate from waist, neck, and height (plus hip circumference for women), making it a practical proxy when clinical equipment isn't available.

BMR and daily calorie needs

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest just to keep vital functions running — breathing, circulation, cell repair. This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990 and repeatedly validated in later research as more accurate than the older Harris-Benedict formula for most modern populations. Multiplying BMR by an activity factor produces your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) — the more practically useful number, since it reflects calories burned across a full day including movement and exercise, not just at rest.

Planning weight loss safely

Since roughly 7,700 kcal corresponds to about 1 kg of body weight, a sustained daily deficit of 500 kcal below your TDEE produces roughly 0.5 kg of loss per week — a widely recommended, sustainable pace that tends to preserve more muscle mass than aggressive crash diets. This calculator's Weight Goal Planner lets you choose a pace and shows both the estimated timeline and the daily calorie adjustment involved, along with a warning if your resulting target dips below commonly cited safe minimums (roughly 1,200 kcal/day for women and 1,500 kcal/day for men as general population floors, not personalized medical advice).

Planning weight gain safely

For weight gain, a moderate surplus of 250–500 kcal/day above TDEE, combined with resistance training, tends to favor lean muscle gain over fat gain compared to a large, rapid surplus. The same Weight Goal Planner works in reverse for gain goals — just set a goal weight above your current weight and choose a pace.

Exercise recommendations

The WHO recommends adults get at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity) per week, plus muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days per week. Combining cardiovascular exercise with resistance training tends to produce better body-composition outcomes than either alone — particularly relevant if your BMI and body fat estimate disagree, which usually signals a body composition worth addressing through strength training rather than weight loss alone.

Nutrition tips for a healthy weight

Sustainable weight management tends to favor whole foods, adequate protein (roughly 1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight for most active adults), fiber-rich carbohydrates, and consistent meal timing over restrictive fad diets. Tracking calories loosely against your TDEE estimate from this calculator — rather than guessing — is one of the most reliable ways to hit a target rate of change without needing extreme restriction.

When to consult a doctor

See a healthcare professional if your BMI sits outside the normal range alongside other symptoms, if you have a family history of weight-related conditions like diabetes or heart disease, before starting any significant weight-loss or weight-gain program, or if you are pregnant, an athlete, an older adult, or managing an existing medical condition — situations where BMI alone is least reliable on its own. This tool is an educational screening aid, not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.

WHO guidelines on BMI

The World Health Organization defines the global adult BMI cutoffs used throughout this calculator (18.5, 25, 30, 35, 40) and explicitly frames BMI as a population-level screening measure, not an individual diagnostic tool. WHO also publishes the separate Asia-Pacific BMI cutoffs (23 for overweight, 27.5 for obesity) in recognition of research showing increased cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI values in South Asian and other Asian populations.

CDC guidelines on BMI

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention uses the same core adult categories as WHO and provides BMI-for-age growth charts for children and teens (2–19 years), reflecting that healthy body fat percentage changes substantially through childhood and adolescence. The CDC also explicitly cautions that BMI is a screening tool correlated with more direct body-fat measures, not a direct measurement of body fat itself.

BMI considerations for Indian and South Asian populations

Multiple studies on South Asian populations, including in India, have found higher body fat percentage and greater visceral (abdominal) fat at the same BMI compared with European populations — sometimes summarized as the "thin-fat Indian" phenotype. This is the basis for the WHO Asia-Pacific guideline lowering the overweight threshold to 23 and obesity to 27.5. This calculator includes an "Asia-Pacific (Indian) BMI cutoffs" toggle in Advanced Options so you can view your category under either standard.

Common BMI mistakes

  • Mixing units — entering pounds into a kilograms field (or vice versa) silently produces a nonsensical BMI.
  • Using outdated height — adults can lose 1–2 cm of height by their senior years, subtly skewing a years-old measurement.
  • Applying adult categories to children — pediatric BMI requires age- and sex-specific percentile charts, not the fixed adult cutoffs.
  • Treating BMI as a diagnosis — a single BMI reading is a screening signal, not a medical verdict on your health.
  • Ignoring body composition context — athletes and very lean but low-muscle individuals can both be misclassified by BMI alone.
  • Applying standard categories during pregnancy — expected gestational weight gain is not the same as unhealthy weight gain.

BMI for children and teens (reference only)

Pediatric BMI is calculated with the same formula as adult BMI, but interpreted completely differently: rather than fixed cutoffs, a child's BMI is plotted against CDC or WHO growth charts as a percentile relative to other children of the same age and sex, since a "healthy" BMI value at age 6 is very different from a healthy value at age 16. This calculator is designed and validated for adults aged 18 and up — use a pediatrician-reviewed growth chart tool for children.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is BMI (Body Mass Index)?

BMI is a simple screening measurement that estimates body fatness from your weight and height, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. It's used worldwide by organizations like the WHO and CDC as a quick population-level indicator, not a diagnostic tool.

What is the BMI formula?

The metric formula is BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². The imperial formula is BMI = 703 × weight (lb) ÷ height (in)². Both formulas produce the same BMI value for the same body — only the unit conversion constant differs.

How do I calculate BMI in metric units?

Divide your weight in kilograms by your height in metres, then divide again by the same height in metres. For example, 70 kg ÷ (1.70 m × 1.70 m) = 24.2.

How do I calculate BMI in imperial units?

Multiply your weight in pounds by 703, then divide by your height in inches squared. For example, a 154 lb person at 67 inches: (703 × 154) ÷ (67 × 67) ≈ 24.1.

What are the BMI categories?

WHO defines Underweight as below 18.5, Normal weight as 18.5–24.9, Overweight as 25–29.9, and Obesity as 30 and above, further split into Class I (30–34.9), Class II (35–39.9), and Class III (40+).

What BMI is considered healthy?

A BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is classified as the healthy/normal range by WHO for most adults, though the healthy range narrows to roughly 18.5–22.9 under the WHO Asia-Pacific cutoffs sometimes recommended for South Asian populations.

What is a healthy weight range for my height?

Your healthy weight range is the weight span that keeps your BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 (or 18.5–22.9 under Asian cutoffs) at your current height. This calculator computes both ends of that range automatically and shows where your current weight sits on the scale.

What is my ideal weight, and how is it calculated here?

This tool averages four validated formulas — Devine, Robinson, Miller, and Hamwi — which estimate ideal body weight from height and sex. Averaging multiple formulas gives a more balanced estimate than relying on any single one.

Is BMI different for men and women?

The BMI formula and category thresholds are the same for men and women. However, women naturally carry more essential body fat than men at the same BMI, which is why this calculator's body fat, ideal weight, and BMR estimates use separate validated formulas for each sex.

Is BMI accurate for older adults?

BMI tends to under-represent body fat in older adults because muscle mass typically declines with age while fat mass may stay stable or increase, sometimes called sarcopenic obesity. Waist circumference and body composition measures are useful complements for adults over 65.

Is BMI accurate for athletes and muscular people?

No, not reliably. Muscle is denser than fat, so athletes and heavily muscled individuals can have a BMI in the overweight or obese range while carrying very low body fat. Enable Athlete Mode in Advanced Options to see a caveat, and rely more on the waist-based and body fat estimates in that case.

Can I use BMI during pregnancy?

Standard BMI categories don't apply during pregnancy because healthy weight gain is expected and varies by trimester and pre-pregnancy weight. Enable Pregnancy Mode to disable category interpretation — always follow your obstetrician's specific gestational weight gain guidance instead.

What are the limitations of BMI?

BMI doesn't distinguish muscle from fat, doesn't account for fat distribution, and can misclassify athletes, older adults, and some ethnic groups. It's a useful population-level screening tool, not an individual diagnostic measure — pair it with waist measurements and a clinical assessment where possible.

What is waist-to-height ratio and why does it matter?

Waist-to-height ratio (waist ÷ height) is a simple proxy for abdominal fat, which is more strongly linked to cardiometabolic risk than total body weight. A widely cited rule of thumb is to keep your waist circumference below half your height.

What is waist-to-hip ratio?

Waist-to-hip ratio (waist ÷ hip) indicates whether fat is stored more around the abdomen (apple shape) or hips/thighs (pear shape). WHO considers a ratio above 0.90 in men or 0.85 in women to indicate increased health risk.

Does BMI measure body fat?

No. BMI only uses weight and height — it cannot distinguish fat mass from muscle, bone, or water weight. It correlates with body fat at a population level but can be misleading for individuals with atypical body composition.

How is body fat percentage estimated on this page?

This tool uses the U.S. Navy circumference method, which estimates body fat from waist, neck, and height (plus hip for women) using validated logarithmic formulas. It's a reasonably accurate tape-measure estimate, though not as precise as a DEXA scan or hydrostatic weighing.

What is BMR and how is it calculated?

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is the energy your body needs at complete rest to maintain basic functions. This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, widely regarded as the most accurate predictive formula for BMR, based on your weight, height, age, and sex.

What is TDEE and how is it different from BMR?

Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) is your BMR multiplied by an activity factor that accounts for exercise and daily movement. BMR alone reflects resting energy needs; TDEE estimates the calories you actually burn in a full day, which is the number to use for weight management.

How many calories should I eat to lose weight?

A deficit of about 500 kcal/day below your TDEE typically produces roughly 0.5 kg (about 1 lb) of weight loss per week, since roughly 7,700 kcal corresponds to 1 kg of body weight. Always avoid extreme deficits without medical supervision.

How many calories should I eat to gain weight?

A surplus of roughly 250–500 kcal/day above your TDEE supports gradual, mostly lean weight gain of about 0.25–0.5 kg per week; larger surpluses tend to add more fat relative to muscle.

Is BMI different for Indian or South Asian populations?

Research shows South Asians tend to develop cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI values than the global WHO cutoffs suggest, largely due to a tendency toward higher body fat percentage at the same BMI. The WHO Asia-Pacific guidelines lower the overweight cutoff to 23 and obesity to 27.5 — this calculator lets you switch to those cutoffs in Advanced Options.

What do the WHO and CDC say about BMI?

Both the World Health Organization and the U.S. CDC use the same core adult BMI categories (Underweight, Normal, Overweight, Obese) as a population-level screening tool, while explicitly noting that BMI does not directly measure body fat and should be interpreted alongside other health indicators.

What are common BMI calculation mistakes?

Common errors include mixing units, using outdated height, forgetting that BMI categories don't apply to children (who use age- and sex-specific percentile charts) or pregnant women, and treating a single BMI reading as a medical diagnosis rather than a screening signal.
