How EMI Is Calculated

What Is EMI?

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment — a fixed amount you pay every month to repay a loan. It combines both principal repayment and interest so that, by the end of your tenure, the loan is fully closed. Instead of paying interest separately from principal, an EMI bundles both into one predictable number that stays constant for the life of a fixed-rate loan, which is what makes budgeting around a home, car, or personal loan practical. For example, a ₹5,00,000 personal loan at 12% p.a. over 4 years produces a fixed EMI of roughly ₹13,166 every month for 48 months — some months that payment leans more towards interest, some more towards principal, but the number you write on the cheque never changes. Nearly every bank, NBFC, and fintech app — including HDFC, ICICI, SBI, Axis Bank, BankBazaar, PaisaBazaar, Bajaj Finserv, and Groww — uses the same reducing-balance formula shown below.

The EMI Formula Explained

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n / ((1 + r)n − 1)

P is the loan principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and n is the total number of monthly instalments. Each month, interest is charged on the outstanding balance, and whatever remains of the EMI goes towards reducing the principal — which is why early EMIs are interest-heavy and later ones are principal-heavy. This calculator runs that exact formula on every keystroke, then walks the resulting EMI forward month by month to build the full amortization schedule you see further down the page, so the numbers you see are never rounded estimates.

Simple Interest vs Compound Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal for the entire tenure, so it grows linearly and is rarely used for consumer loans — though some lenders still advertise a low "flat rate" that is actually a simple-interest quote in disguise. Compound interest — the basis of the EMI formula — is calculated on the outstanding balance each period, meaning interest itself can attract interest if unpaid, but also that your interest liability shrinks as the balance shrinks. This distinction matters more than most borrowers realize: a ₹1,00,000 loan at a quoted 10% flat rate over 3 years works out to ₹30,000 in interest under simple interest, but the equivalent 10% p.a. on a monthly reducing balance — the method this calculator and virtually every regulated Indian lender actually use for EMIs — costs only about ₹16,162 in interest for the same principal, rate, and tenure. Always confirm whether a quoted rate is flat or reducing before comparing offers; the headline number can look identical while the true cost differs by nearly double.

Fixed vs Floating Interest Rate

A fixed rate stays the same for part or all of your tenure, giving you predictable EMIs regardless of market movements — useful for budgeting but usually priced slightly higher than floating rates. A floating rate is linked to an external benchmark (like the repo rate or a lender's internal cost of funds) and moves up or down periodically; your EMI or tenure adjusts accordingly. Most home loans in India are floating, while personal and car loans are commonly fixed.

Factors That Affect Your EMI

  • Loan amount: A higher principal directly increases EMI, all else equal — which is why right-sizing your borrowing to actual need, not maximum eligibility, is the single biggest lever you control.
  • Interest rate: Even a 0.5% difference can change total interest by lakhs on a large, long-tenure loan, so it's worth negotiating or shopping across lenders rather than accepting the first offer.
  • Tenure: Longer tenure lowers EMI but raises total interest paid; shorter tenure does the opposite — see the tenure comparison table above for real numbers.
  • Credit score: A higher CIBIL score typically unlocks lower interest rates from lenders, since it signals lower default risk; scores above 750 usually get the best offers.
  • Compounding frequency: Daily reducing balance (used by some NBFCs and gold loans) yields a marginally higher effective rate than monthly reducing balance for the same quoted annual rate.
  • Processing fees and charges: These don't change your EMI, but they raise the true cost of the loan — always compare the total cost, not just the EMI, across lenders.
  • Down payment: A larger upfront payment shrinks the financed principal directly, lowering both EMI and total interest for the same rate and tenure.
  • Co-applicant income: Adding a co-applicant's income can increase eligibility and sometimes secure a better rate, since it improves the lender's repayment-capacity assessment.

How Loan Tenure Affects EMI

Tenure has a non-linear effect on EMI: stretching it lowers your monthly outgo but disproportionately increases total interest, because the lender earns interest on a large outstanding balance for far longer. Here's what a ₹10,00,000 loan at 9% p.a. looks like across common tenures:

  • 10 years: EMI ≈ ₹12,668 · Total interest ≈ ₹5,20,109
  • 15 years: EMI ≈ ₹10,143 · Total interest ≈ ₹8,25,680
  • 20 years: EMI ≈ ₹8,997 · Total interest ≈ ₹11,59,342
  • 25 years: EMI ≈ ₹8,392 · Total interest ≈ ₹15,17,589
  • 30 years: EMI ≈ ₹8,046 · Total interest ≈ ₹18,96,641

Going from 10 to 30 years cuts the EMI by roughly 37%, but total interest rises by well over 3.6×. That's why it's worth choosing the shortest tenure your monthly budget comfortably supports rather than the longest one you're offered — use the slider above to compare tenures side by side, and check the Prepayment tool to see how extra payments can claw back years even after you've committed to a longer schedule.

Loan Repayment Strategy

A sound repayment strategy starts before you sign the loan agreement. First, size the loan to what you need rather than the maximum you're offered — lenders approve based on eligibility, not necessity. Second, front-load extra payments whenever possible: because interest accrues on the outstanding balance, prepaying in years one and two saves far more than the same amount prepaid in year fifteen of a twenty-year loan. Third, treat your amortization schedule as a living document — revisit it whenever your income changes, a lender revises rates, or you receive a windfall, and re-run the numbers here to decide whether to reduce tenure, reduce EMI, or refinance. Finally, keep a buffer: automate EMI debits from an account that always holds at least one extra instalment, so a timing mismatch never turns into a missed payment and a credit score hit.

Benefits of Prepayment

Because interest is charged on the outstanding balance, any extra payment — whether a small recurring top-up or an annual bonus lump sum — directly reduces the principal that future interest is calculated on. Prepaying early in the tenure has an outsized effect since more of the loan life remains. Use the Prepayment tool above to see your exact interest savings and revised payoff date; most lenders in India do not charge prepayment penalties on floating-rate home loans.

How to Reduce Total Interest

  1. Make a larger down payment to shrink the financed principal.
  2. Choose the shortest tenure your EMI budget allows.
  3. Negotiate a lower rate using your credit score and relationship with the lender, or switch via balance transfer.
  4. Prepay opportunistically with bonuses, tax refunds, or maturing investments.
  5. Avoid unnecessary add-on loans or top-ups that restart your interest clock.

Common Loan Mistakes to Avoid

  • Borrowing the maximum eligible amount instead of what you actually need — a higher sanction doesn't mean you should use all of it.
  • Ignoring processing fees, GST, and insurance when comparing lenders, which can make a "lower rate" offer more expensive overall once charges are added in.
  • Choosing tenure based only on the lowest EMI, without checking total interest — use the tenure slider here to see the full trade-off before committing.
  • Skipping the amortization schedule and not knowing how much of each EMI covers interest versus principal, which makes it hard to judge whether prepaying now is worthwhile.
  • Not checking prepayment or foreclosure charges before signing the loan agreement — some lenders levy 2–4% on fixed-rate loan foreclosure.
  • Skipping multiple lender quotes and accepting the first pre-approved offer, which is rarely the most competitive one available to your credit profile.

How the Amortization Schedule Helps You Plan

An amortization schedule is the month-by-month (and year-by-year) ledger of your loan: how much of each EMI goes to interest, how much reduces principal, and what balance remains after every payment. It matters for three practical reasons. First, it shows you exactly when your principal-to-interest ratio flips in your favor, which helps you time prepayments for maximum effect. Second, it's the document you'll need for tax purposes — home loan interest and principal are claimed under different sections, and your lender's provisional certificate is built from exactly this kind of table. Third, it lets you sanity-check any lender's numbers independently; the schedule generated below this calculator recomputes from the same formula shown above, month by month, so you can verify a bank's offer before signing. Use the search box to jump to a specific year, expand any row to see individual months, and export the full table to CSV whenever you need it for a spreadsheet or loan file.

Loan Eligibility and Affordability

Before you shop for a loan, it helps to know what you can actually afford — not just what a lender might approve. Most Indian banks and NBFCs use FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio): the share of your net monthly income already committed to EMIs and other fixed obligations, typically capped at 40–50%. The Eligibility calculator above applies this same logic in reverse — enter your income, existing EMIs, and target FOIR, and it works backward through the EMI formula to show your maximum affordable EMI and the maximum loan amount that EMI supports at your expected rate and tenure. Treating this as a ceiling rather than a target is good practice: approving yourself for less than the maximum leaves room for rate hikes, income disruptions, and other financial goals.

Financial Planning Tips for Loan Repayment

Keep your total EMI obligations (including this loan) under 40–50% of your net monthly income to maintain healthy cash flow — this is the same FOIR ratio lenders use, and staying comfortably below it gives you a buffer against future rate increases. Build an emergency fund covering 3–6 months of EMIs before taking on a large loan, so a temporary income gap never forces a missed payment. Review your loan annually: if market rates fall or your credit score improves, a balance transfer could meaningfully cut your interest cost — run the numbers through the Comparison tool above before switching. Finally, automate EMI payments to avoid missed instalments, which can hurt your credit score and attract penal interest, and revisit your amortization schedule every time your income or goals change.

Smart Loan Repayment Strategies

📅 Align EMI date with salary

Set your EMI due date a few days after your salary credit, not before, to avoid bounced payments, penal charges, and unnecessary damage to your credit score.

💰 Prepay with windfalls

Direct bonuses, tax refunds, and maturing FDs towards prepayment instead of new discretionary spending — the Prepayment tool above shows exactly how much interest that decision saves.

📉 Track rate resets

For floating-rate loans, review your rate at every reset cycle and proactively ask your lender for a rate reduction if your credit profile or repayment history has improved.

🔄 Consider balance transfer

If a competing lender offers a meaningfully lower rate, use the comparison tool above to check whether the lifetime interest savings outweigh processing and transfer costs.

🧮 Recalculate before signing

Always verify the lender's EMI quote using an independent calculator like this one before signing loan documents — small formula or fee differences add up over a long tenure.

🛡️ Insure the loan

Consider loan protection insurance so your family isn't left carrying the EMI burden alone in case of an unforeseen loss of income or life event.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is EMI and how does it work?

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment — a fixed amount you repay every month towards a loan, covering both principal and interest, until the loan is fully repaid over the agreed tenure.

What is the formula to calculate EMI?

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ / ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), where P is the loan principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and n is the number of monthly instalments.

Reduce tenure or reduce EMI after prepayment — what's the difference?

Reducing tenure keeps your EMI the same but shortens how long you pay, saving more interest overall. Reducing EMI keeps your original tenure but lowers your monthly instalment, easing cash flow instead.

How does loan tenure affect my EMI?

A longer tenure lowers your monthly EMI but increases total interest paid over the life of the loan. A shorter tenure raises the EMI but reduces total interest significantly.

What is a good home loan interest rate in India?

Home loan rates for salaried borrowers with good credit typically range from 8% to 9.5% p.a. depending on the lender, loan amount, and credit score. Compare offers before finalizing.

What is the difference between fixed and floating interest rates?

A fixed rate stays constant for the loan tenure, giving predictable EMIs. A floating rate moves with the market or lender's benchmark rate, so your EMI or tenure can change over time.
