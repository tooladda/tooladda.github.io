1. What is PDF page extraction?

PDF page extraction is the process of copying selected pages out of a PDF document and saving them as a brand-new PDF file. The key word is copying. Extraction is non-destructive: your source document is opened read-only, never written to, and comes out of the process with every page exactly where it was. What you get back is a second, smaller file containing only the pages you asked for.

That distinction matters more than it sounds, because three different operations get confused with each other constantly:

  • Extracting takes the pages you choose and produces one new document containing just those pages. The original is untouched. This is what you want when someone asks you to "send me just the signature page."
  • Splitting divides one document into several separate files — for example, turning a 90-page report into nine 10-page chunks. Use our Split PDF tool when that's the goal.
  • Deleting pages produces a document that is the original minus the pages you removed. You can achieve the same result here by selecting everything you want to keep and extracting that.

Technically, a good extractor copies page objects rather than re-rendering them. Each PDF page is a container that references fonts, images, vector drawing instructions and metadata. A proper extraction carries those references across intact, which is why the output looks identical to the source and why the text remains selectable and searchable. A poor extractor — or a tool that converts pages to images along the way — will produce a file that looks superficially right but has lost its text layer, ballooned in size, and become unsearchable. This tool copies at the object level, so none of that happens.

2. How to extract pages from PDF online

The full workflow takes under a minute:

  1. Upload your PDF. Drag it onto the box or click to browse. It is read on your device — there is no upload progress bar because there is no upload.
  2. Check the file details. The tool immediately shows the file name, size and total page count, so you can confirm you grabbed the right document before doing anything else.
  3. Look at the page thumbnails. Every page is rendered as a preview so you are selecting what you can see, rather than guessing at page numbers. Thumbnails load lazily as you scroll, so even a 400-page document stays fast.

If you prefer the keyboard, the thumbnail grid is fully navigable: arrow keys move between pages, Space or Enter toggles selection, and holding Shift while using the arrow keys extends the selection — the same conventions you already know from a file manager.

3. Why extract selected PDF pages?

Nearly every reason comes down to the same thing: you need to give someone part of a document without giving them all of it. Sending a 200-page master agreement when the recipient asked for the pricing annexe is at best unhelpful and at worst a disclosure problem.

Share less, not more

Send only the clause, the invoice or the certificate that was asked for, instead of an entire file full of unrelated information.

Cut the file size

Three pages out of three hundred is a fraction of the bytes — often the difference between an email that sends and one that bounces.

Meet upload limits

Portals for tax filings, visa applications and job applications frequently cap uploads at a few megabytes or a set number of pages.

Redact by omission

The safest way to hide a page is not to include it. Extraction removes the page entirely rather than covering it up.

Reuse without rebuilding

Pull a chart, a template page or an appendix out of an old document to drop into a new one.

Archive what matters

Keep the two pages that have long-term value instead of storing the whole bundle forever.

4. Extract one page from a PDF

The single most common request. Click the page you want in the thumbnail grid — or type just its number in the range box — and extract. You get a one-page PDF containing exactly that page, with its original fonts, layout, images and quality intact.

Two practical notes. First, check the page number carefully if you're typing it. PDF page numbers here are physical positions in the file, counting from 1 — they are not the numbers printed on the page. A report with a cover and two roman-numeral front-matter pages will have "page 1" of its printed body sitting at physical page 4. This is exactly why the thumbnails exist: select what you can see and the ambiguity disappears.

Second, if the page you want is a scan, extraction preserves it perfectly but does not make it searchable — a scanned page contains an image, not text, and copying it doesn't change that. That's expected behaviour, not a fault in the extraction.

5. Extract multiple pages from a PDF

Selected pages do not have to be next to each other. Click page 2, then page 9, then page 40 and all three are collected. By default they are written into the new PDF in page order, so the output reads naturally regardless of the order you clicked.

If you specifically want them in the order you picked — say you're assembling a short brief where the summary should come first even though it sits at the back of the source document — choose the In my click order output option. Each selected page shows a small numbered badge indicating its position in that sequence, so you can see the running order before you commit.

For scanned documents, the Odd pages and Even pages buttons are worth knowing. Feeding a stack of double-sided sheets through a single-sided scanner produces a file with all the fronts followed by all the backs, or fronts and backs interleaved. One click separates them.

6. Extract page ranges from a PDF

The range box accepts the notation you'd write by hand. 4-9 gives you six consecutive pages. 1-3, 7, 12-15 mixes ranges and single pages in one expression. Whitespace is ignored, so 1 - 3 , 7 works identically, and duplicates are collapsed automatically — asking for 2-5, 3 does not put page 3 in twice.

Typing into the range box and clicking thumbnails are two views of the same selection, kept in sync in both directions. Type a range and the matching thumbnails light up; click a few more pages and the box updates to describe what you now have. That means you can start with a rough range, then refine it by eye — which is usually faster than getting the numbers exactly right first time.

For a contiguous block, shift-click is often quickest of all: click the first page, then shift-click the last, and everything between them is selected.

7. Save selected pages as a new PDF

The output is a genuinely new PDF document, not a modified copy of your original. The file is named after your source document with a page summary appended — so extracting pages 3 to 5 from contract.pdf gives you contract-pages-3-5.pdf, which stays meaningful in a downloads folder six months later.

What carries over: page content, fonts, embedded images, vector graphics, page dimensions and rotation. What does not carry over: document-level features that belong to the file as a whole rather than to individual pages — bookmarks pointing at pages you didn't select, form-field data, and the original document's metadata. This is normal for page extraction in any tool, and it's usually what you want: a clean, self-contained subset with no dangling references to pages that aren't there.

If you need the extracted pages to be locked down before sending, run the result through Protect PDF. If you need them flattened so form fields and annotations can't be edited, use Flatten PDF.

9. Business use cases

In an office, extraction is usually about controlling what leaves the building. Procurement teams pull the commercial terms out of a master agreement for the finance approver, who has no business reading the technical schedules. Sales teams extract the two slides that matter from a fifty-slide deck for a follow-up email. HR extracts a single policy from a two-hundred-page handbook so a new starter can actually find it.

Accounts payable is a particularly heavy user: supplier statements arrive as one long PDF and each invoice needs to become a separate document to attach to its own ledger entry. The each page separately output handles that in one pass and returns everything as a ZIP, rather than making you extract, download and rename thirty times.

There is also a compliance angle. Under data-minimisation principles found in GDPR and similar regimes, sharing only the pages a recipient actually needs is not just tidier — it is the defensible choice. Extraction that never uploads the source document keeps that whole exercise inside your own perimeter.

10. Student and education use cases

Students extract pages constantly, usually to make something large into something readable. Pull the three chapters that are on the syllabus out of a whole textbook PDF so the file opens quickly on a phone. Take last year's past-paper questions out of a combined examinations bundle. Extract the reading assigned for one seminar from a compiled course pack, so the tablet you take to class isn't carrying four hundred pages.

Teachers work in the other direction: extract a single worksheet from a resource pack to hand out, or pull the answer key out before distributing the questions — a case where extraction is a genuine safeguard, because a hidden or covered-up page can still be recovered, while a page that was never in the file cannot.

For submissions, extraction is often mandatory rather than convenient. University portals commonly cap uploads by size or page count, and a dissertation appendix routinely has to be submitted as a separate document from the main body.

11. Legal and office document use cases

Legal work runs on precise page references, which makes extraction a daily tool. Pull the specific exhibit a court or counterparty asked for out of a closing bundle. Extract the executed signature pages from a document set to circulate proof of execution. Take one clause and its definitions out of a master agreement for a query, without disclosing commercially sensitive schedules elsewhere in the file.

The privilege point is the important one. Sending a whole document when only one page was requested can waive confidentiality over material you never intended to share, and once it's sent it cannot be recalled. Extracting exactly the requested pages — and doing so in a tool where the source file never leaves your device — keeps both the disclosure and the processing inside the boundary you control.

In general office administration the same logic applies to certificates, licences, insurance schedules and registration documents: extract the page that proves the thing, send that, keep the rest.

12. Extracting PDF pages on mobile

Documents arrive as email attachments on phones far more often than on desktops, so extracting on mobile should not be an afterthought. Here it isn't: the thumbnail grid reflows into a compact layout sized for thumbs, tapping a page selects it, and a sticky bar pinned to the bottom of the screen keeps the running page count and the Extract button visible while you scroll through a long document — so you never have to scroll back up to find the button.

Two tips for large files on a phone. Use the thumbnail size control to shrink previews, which fits more pages on screen and lightens rendering. And prefer the range box for documents of a few hundred pages: typing 120-135 is faster than scrolling to page 120, and the thumbnails will confirm your choice afterwards.

Because everything runs locally, extraction also works on a flaky connection or none at all — once the page has loaded, no network is needed to finish the job.

13. Privacy and file security

Most online PDF tools work by uploading your document to a server, processing it there, and letting you download the result. That means your file is transmitted across the internet, written to storage you don't control, in a jurisdiction you probably haven't checked, under a retention policy you almost certainly haven't read. For a holiday brochure, fine. For a payslip, a medical letter or a signed contract, considerably less so.

This tool avoids the entire category of risk by never uploading anything:

  • Your PDF is read with the browser's file API and stays in the tab's memory.
  • Page rendering and page copying both execute as JavaScript on your device.
  • No network request carries file content. You can verify this yourself — open your browser's developer tools, switch to the Network tab, and extract a page. You'll see the page's own assets load and nothing else.
  • Closing or reloading the tab discards everything. There is no server-side copy, so there is nothing to expire, leak or subpoena.
  • Once loaded, the tool keeps working offline — a practical demonstration that no server is involved in the work itself.

This is what makes it usable for material covered by NDAs, professional privilege, HIPAA, GDPR or an internal data-classification policy that forbids third-party document processing. Check your own organisation's rules as always — but "the file never leaves the device" is a much shorter conversation with a security team than "they say they delete it after an hour."

This tool covers the same core task with no licence, no installer and no admin rights: open the page in whatever browser you already have, select, extract, close the tab. It runs on a Chromebook, on a managed corporate desktop and on a phone.

The honest limits, so you know before you rely on it: extraction does not add OCR, so a scanned page stays an image; document-level bookmarks and form data don't survive extraction (as in any extractor); and very large files are bounded by your device's memory rather than by a server farm. Will my PDF quality change?

No. Pages are copied at the object level rather than re-rendered or re-compressed, so text stays selectable and searchable, images keep their original resolution, and vector graphics stay sharp. The extracted pages are the same content as in the source document.

Yes. A scanned PDF is just pages containing images, and extraction copies those pages exactly as they are, at unchanged quality. Note that extraction doesn't add a text layer — if you need searchable text, run OCR separately.

Yes. Your PDF is read and processed entirely inside your browser. No file or page is ever transmitted, so there's no upload to intercept and no server-side copy to retain. The page is served over HTTPS and keeps working offline once loaded.

Extracting pulls the specific pages you choose into one new PDF, leaving the original whole — use it when you want a clean subset to send to someone. Splitting divides a document into several separate files. If that's what you need, use Split PDF instead.

No. Extraction is a copy operation and is completely non-destructive — your source file is opened read-only and never written to. If you want a document without certain pages, select all the pages you want to keep and extract those; the result is your new file.
