What is this online coin flip game?

This free coin flip tool simulates a real coin toss for quick decisions, games, classroom activities, and fun. Each flip uses fair randomness and saves your stats locally so you can track heads versus tails over time.

Frequently asked questions

Is a digital coin flip actually fair?

It is a reasonable thing to be suspicious about, so here is the honest answer. Where your browser provides one — which every current browser does — the flip uses the cryptographic random number generator, the same source used to create encryption keys, falling back to the ordinary generator only on very old browsers. Each flip is independent and each side has an equal chance.

In one respect it is fairer than a real coin. Physical coins are not perfectly balanced, and a well-known study of coin tossing found a small but genuine bias toward the side that started face up, because a tossed coin precesses slightly rather than rotating cleanly. A digital flip has no physics to bias it.

Why you keep seeing runs of the same side

Flip a fair coin twenty times and a run of four or five identical results is not unusual — it is expected. People routinely read those runs as evidence that something is rigged, when they are exactly what randomness looks like.

The reason is that a coin has no memory. After five heads, the chance of heads next is still one half. The belief that a tails is somehow "due" is called the gambler's fallacy, and it is one of the most reliable errors in human reasoning.

What does hold is that proportions settle down over many flips. Ten flips can easily land 8–2. A thousand flips will sit close to 500–500, not because the coin corrects itself, but because early runs get diluted by everything that follows.

Good reasons to flip a coin

Settling something trivial

Who pays, who goes first, which restaurant. A coin ends a pointless debate in one second.

Breaking a genuine tie

When two options are honestly equal, deciding at random is faster and fairer than arguing.

Finding out what you wanted

The most useful trick with a coin: flip it, and notice how you feel about the result. Disappointment tells you your real preference.

Teaching probability

Flip fifty times and watch the running totals. It is the clearest demonstration of why streaks are normal.

When not to use a coin

A coin is for decisions where you genuinely do not mind either outcome. If one option matters more than the other, flipping does not remove the responsibility — it just postpones it. Use a coin to break ties, not to avoid thinking.

It is also not a substitute for a proper random draw when stakes are involved. For picking a winner from a list, use a tool designed for that, where every entry has an equal chance and the process can be shown to everyone.

Why do I keep getting the same result several times in a row?

Because that is what randomness looks like. In twenty flips, a run of four or five identical results is expected rather than suspicious. People tend to read streaks as evidence of rigging, but genuinely random sequences contain more clustering than most of us intuitively expect.

If I get five heads, is tails more likely next?

No. A coin has no memory, so the chance of tails is still one half. The belief that a result is somehow due is called the gambler's fallacy, and it is one of the most persistent errors in reasoning about chance.

Then why do results even out over time?

They even out in proportion, not in count. Ten flips can easily land 8 to 2. A thousand flips will sit close to half and half — not because the coin corrects itself, but because an early run gets diluted by everything that comes after it.

Can I flip more than once?

Yes, as many times as you like. The running tally shows how the results are splitting, which is the clearest way to watch a proportion settle down as the number of flips grows.

Are my flips saved?

The recent history and counts are kept in your browser while you use the page. Nothing is uploaded to us and nothing is tied to an account.

Can I use this to settle a bet?

For something friendly, yes — that is what it is for. For anything with real money or legal weight, use a method both parties can see and verify in person; nobody should have to trust a web page for that.

Is heads or tails better to call?

Neither. With a fair flip the two are exactly equal, so calling one over the other makes no difference. If you are calling a real coin, there is a very slight edge in calling whichever side is facing up before the toss.

Can I flip a coin more than two ways?

Not with a coin — it has two sides. For three or more options, or for picking a winner from a list where every entry must have an equal chance, use the Random Name Picker instead.

Yes. The coin scales to the screen and you can tap anywhere on it to flip. The history and counts work the same as on a desktop.

Does it work offline?

Yes, once the page has loaded. The flip is generated on your device with no server involved, so it keeps working if your connection drops.

Is it free?

Completely. No sign-up, no download and no limit on how many times you flip.

How should I actually use a coin flip to decide something?

The most useful trick is not the result itself. Flip the coin, and pay attention to how you feel when you see the outcome. If you are disappointed, you already knew which option you wanted — the coin just made you admit it.
