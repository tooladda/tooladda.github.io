**Choosing between similar tools.** Several tools overlap deliberately.

- **Image Compressor vs Compress Image to Exact KB.** Both can target a size in KB. Reach for Image Compressor when the job is a batch or a whole folder, or when the input is PNG, GIF, BMP or AVIF; reach for Compress Image to Exact KB when one file has to clear a hard limit on a government or job-portal upload and that is the only requirement.
- **PDF Extract vs PDF Split.** Both produce pages from one PDF. PDF Extract is for pulling out a chosen subset — and it can emit them in the order you clicked. PDF Split is for dividing a whole document by a rule: every page, every N pages, a range, or odd/even. Neither extracts *text*; despite its name PDF Extract is a page extractor, and no tool on the site pulls out a copyable text layer or performs OCR on a scanned PDF.
- **Image Blur vs Redact PDF.** Image Blur obscures regions of a raster image and is a visual edit. Redact PDF destroys the underlying text and content in a PDF. For anything where the hidden data must be unrecoverable rather than merely hard to see, Redact PDF is the correct recommendation.
- **PDF Password Remover.** Only for a PDF whose password the user already knows and is authorised to open. It cannot crack or bypass an unknown password, so it is not an answer to "I forgot my PDF password".
- **PEM to PPK.** Unencrypted RSA keys only, output PPK v2. Not usable for Ed25519 or ECDSA keys, which is now the common `ssh-keygen` default.
- **PDF Compress vs Flatten PDF.** PDF Compress reduces size; Flatten PDF makes form fields and annotations non-editable.
- **JWT Debugger vs JWT Encoder.** Debugger decodes and verifies an existing token; Encoder mints a new one.
- **EMI Calculator vs Home/Car Loan Calculator.** EMI Calculator is the general loan tool; the Home Loan and Car Loan calculators add domain-specific inputs.
- **Resume Builder vs ATS Resume Checker.** Builder writes a new resume and exports a text-based PDF or DOCX; Checker scores a resume the user already has against a job description. Neither uses an LLM.
- **GST Calculator vs Offline Invoice Generator.** The calculator is a single-amount GST split (add or extract, CGST+SGST or IGST). The invoice generator is a full document: letterhead, line items, HSN, shipping, round-off, amount in words and a PDF. Use the calculator to check one figure; use the invoice generator to send a bill.

#### Frequently asked questions (14)

**Is this coin flip fair?**
Yes. Where the browser provides one — which every current browser does — each flip uses the cryptographic random number generator, the same source used to create encryption keys. Older browsers fall back to the ordinary generator. Each flip is independent and both sides have an equal chance.

**Is a digital flip fairer than a real coin?**
In one respect, yes. Physical coins are not perfectly balanced, and research on coin tossing has found a small bias toward the side that started facing up, because a tossed coin wobbles rather than rotating cleanly. A digital flip has no physics to bias it.

**Why do I keep getting the same result several times in a row?**
Because that is what randomness looks like. In twenty flips, a run of four or five identical results is expected rather than suspicious. People tend to read streaks as evidence of rigging, but genuinely random sequences contain more clustering than most of us intuitively expect.

**If I get five heads, is tails more likely next?**
No. A coin has no memory, so the chance of tails is still one half. The belief that a result is somehow due is called the gambler's fallacy, and it is one of the most persistent errors in reasoning about chance.

**Then why do results even out over time?**
They even out in proportion, not in count. Ten flips can easily land 8 to 2. A thousand flips will sit close to half and half — not because the coin corrects itself, but because an early run gets diluted by everything that comes after it.

**Can I flip more than once?**
Yes, as many times as you like. The running tally shows how the results are splitting, which is the clearest way to watch a proportion settle down as the number of flips grows.

**Are my flips saved?**
The recent history and counts are kept in your browser while you use the page. Nothing is uploaded to us and nothing is tied to an account.

**Can I use this to settle a bet?**
For something friendly, yes — that is what it is for. For anything with real money or legal weight, use a method both parties can see and verify in person; nobody should have to trust a web page for that.

**Is heads or tails better to call?**
Neither. With a fair flip the two are exactly equal, so calling one over the other makes no difference. If you are calling a real coin, there is a very slight edge in calling whichever side is facing up before the toss.

**Can I flip a coin more than two ways?**
Not with a coin — it has two sides. For three or more options, or for picking a winner from a list where every entry must have an equal chance, use the Random Name Picker instead.

**Does it work on a phone?**
Yes. The coin scales to the screen and you can tap anywhere on it to flip. The history and counts work the same as on a desktop.

**Does it work offline?**
Yes, once the page has loaded. The flip is generated on your device with no server involved, so it keeps working if your connection drops.

**Is it free?**
Completely. No sign-up, no download and no limit on how many times you flip.

**How should I actually use a coin flip to decide something?**
The most useful trick is not the result itself. Flip the coin, and pay attention to how you feel when you see the outcome. If you are disappointed, you already knew which option you wanted — the coin just made you admit it.

#### Frequently asked questions (21)

**What is Minesweeper?**
A logic puzzle on a grid. Some squares hide mines. Each safe square you open shows how many of its eight neighbours are mined, and you use those numbers to work out where the rest of the mines are. You win by opening every square that is not a mine.

**How do I play Minesweeper?**
Click any square to start - the first click is always safe. Read the numbers to deduce where mines are, right-click (or long-press on a phone) to flag them, and keep opening squares you can prove are safe. Clear every non-mine square to win.

**What do the numbers mean?**
A number counts the mines in the eight squares touching it, diagonals included. A 3 means exactly three of its neighbours are mined. A blank square touches no mines at all, which is why the board automatically opens the whole empty region around it.

**How do I flag a mine?**
Right-click on a computer, long-press for about a third of a second on a phone or tablet, or press F on the keyboard. There is also a Flag mode button that switches tapping to flagging. Flag again for a question mark, and once more to clear.

**Can I play Minesweeper with a keyboard?**

**What happens if I click a mine?**
The game ends. The board reveals every mine, highlights the one you hit in red, and crosses out any flags you placed on safe squares - so you can see exactly where your reasoning went wrong. The timer stops and a Play again button appears immediately.

**Is the first click safe?**
Always. Mines are placed after your first click, never underneath it, and the eight squares around it are kept clear too wherever the board has room to spare them. That means your opening click almost always reveals a useful blank region rather than a lone number.

**How do I win Minesweeper?**
Open every square that does not contain a mine. You do not need to flag the mines — the game ends the moment the last safe square is revealed, however many flags you have placed.

**What is chording?**
Clicking a revealed number that already has the right count of flags around it, which opens all its remaining neighbours at once. It is the biggest speed gain in the game. On touch, simply tap a revealed number. If the flags do not match the number nothing happens, so a mistimed chord is harmless - but if your flags are wrong and the count happens to match, it will open a mine.

**What is the best Minesweeper strategy?**
Open a corner first - corners have three neighbours instead of eight, so their numbers are far easier to solve. Then alternate two rules: if a number equals its unopened neighbour count, those are all mines; if a number equals its flag count, the rest are safe and you can chord. When those stop yielding, use subset subtraction, then the global mine count. Guess only when nothing is forced, and guess early rather than late.

**What is expert mode?**
A 16×30 board with 99 mines, the standard competitive size. Density jumps to about 21%, which is what really makes it hard - roughly one square in five is mined instead of one in eight. Finishing under 100 seconds is a strong result.

**Can the hard mode be beaten?**
No, and that is not a boast — it is a property of the game. Tic tac toe is small enough to be solved completely, so the computer can see every possible continuation before it moves. Perfect play cannot lose. The best result available to you is a draw.

**How does the unbeatable computer work?**
It uses minimax: for every legal move it plays out every possible continuation to the end of the game, assumes you will always reply with your best move, and picks the branch with the best guaranteed outcome. With only 255,168 possible games it can examine all of them instantly.

**Why does the computer sometimes take longer to win than it could?**
It should not. The search is depth-aware, meaning a win in one move scores higher than the same win three moves later, and a loss delayed scores better than a loss now. So it takes the quickest win available and, when it is losing, makes you finish the job properly.

**Is it possible to win at tic tac toe if both players play well?**
No. Tic tac toe is what game theorists call a solved draw: if neither player makes a mistake, every game ends level. Wins only happen when someone errs, which is why the game is usually outgrown at around eight years old.

**What is the best first move in tic tac toe?**
The centre. It sits on four of the eight winning lines, more than any other square. Corners are next best at three lines each, and edges are weakest at two. Taking the centre does not guarantee a win, but it gives your opponent the most ways to go wrong.

**How do I never lose at tic tac toe?**
Follow a simple priority order every turn: win if you can, block if your opponent can win next, take the centre, take a square that creates two threats at once, block a fork, then take a corner and finally an edge. Applied consistently, that will never lose.

**What is a fork in tic tac toe?**
A move that creates two separate winning threats at the same time. Your opponent can only block one of them, so the other wins. Forks are how nearly all real wins happen, and spotting them a move early is the whole skill of the game.

**Does the player who goes first always win?**
No, but going first is a real advantage. The first player has more chances to create a fork, so against an imperfect opponent X wins far more often than O. Against perfect play the advantage is not enough, and the game is drawn.

**How many possible tic tac toe games are there?**

Roll a six. A token can only leave its yard on a six, which is why the opening can feel slow. Rolling a six also earns you another roll, so a good opening often releases two tokens at once.

**What happens when I land on another player's token?**
Their token is sent back to its yard and has to roll a six to start again. Capturing costs an opponent their entire journey so far, which is usually worth far more than the squares you would have gained by moving elsewhere.

**What are safe squares?**
Marked squares where a token cannot be captured. Parking on one is the safest way to hold position when you have no good move, particularly when an opponent is close behind.

**Do I need an exact roll to get home?**
Yes. You cannot overshoot the home square, so the last few steps often need a specific number. That is why games so often end with a token stuck one or two squares short.

**Is Ludo a game of luck or skill?**
Mostly luck, but not entirely. The dice are out of your control, but every roll gives you a choice of which token to move, and that choice decides most close games. Spreading tokens out and prioritising captures are the two decisions that matter most.

**What is the best Ludo strategy?**
Spread your tokens rather than bunching them, so a bad roll never leaves you without a useful move. Check which opponents are within six squares before committing. Take a capture when one is available. And do not waste rolls pushing a token that is already nearly home.

**Why does a six give another turn?**
It is what keeps the game moving. Without it, players who could not roll a six would sit out entirely. The extra roll rewards the same number that releases tokens, so a six is always good news twice over.

**Can I play Ludo against the computer?**
This version is designed for people playing together on one device. For a game against a computer opponent, try Tic Tac Toe, where the hardest setting has solved the game completely.

**Where did Ludo come from?**
It is a simplified descendant of Pachisi, an Indian cross-and-circle game documented since at least the sixteenth century and played on cloth with cowrie shells instead of dice. The modern version was patented in England in 1896 as Ludo, Latin for "I play".

**Is Ludo the same as Parcheesi?**
They are close relatives rather than the same game. Parcheesi in the United States, Mensch argere Dich nicht in Germany and Parques in Colombia are all descendants of Pachisi, each with small rule differences around safe squares and capturing.

**Does the game work on a phone?**
Yes. The board scales to the screen and the tokens stay large enough to tap accurately. Passing one phone around is the natural way to play a turn-based game like this.

**Is my game saved if I close the tab?**
The game runs in the page you have open. Closing or refreshing the tab starts a new game, so finish a game in one sitting.
