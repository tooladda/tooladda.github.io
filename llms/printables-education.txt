#### Frequently asked questions (10)

**What is a janam kundali?**
A janam kundali, or kundli, is a map of the sky at the moment and place of a birth, drawn in the way Vedic astrology sets it out. It records the rising sign (lagna), the sign and nakshatra of each of the nine grahas, and the twelve houses counted from the lagna.

**Do I need the exact birth time?**
For the lagna and the houses, yes. The rising sign changes about every two hours, so a birth time that is an hour out can move every house. The Moon's sign and nakshatra are far more forgiving, and the tithi and the planets other than the Moon barely move in a day.

**Which ayanamsa does this kundli maker use?**
Lahiri, also called Chitrapaksha, which is the ayanamsa used by the Indian government's calendar and by most Indian astrologers. It is about 23°51' at the year 2000 and grows by roughly 50 arc seconds a year.

**North Indian or South Indian chart — which should I read?**
They hold the same information. In the North Indian chart the houses stay in fixed positions and the sign numbers move; the lagna is always the diamond at the top. In the South Indian chart the signs stay in fixed boxes and the lagna is marked. Read whichever one you grew up with; the tool draws both.

**What is Vimshottari dasha?**
It is a 120-year cycle of planetary periods. The part of its nakshatra that the Moon had already crossed at birth decides which lord's period was running and how much of it was left, and the rest follow in a fixed order. The tool lists each mahadasha with its antardashas and marks the one running today.

**What is the navamsa (D9) chart?**
The navamsa divides every sign into nine parts of 3°20' and maps each part to a sign of its own. Traditionally it is read alongside the main chart, especially for marriage and for the strength of a planet.

**Is my birth data sent anywhere?**
No. The whole chart is worked out by your own browser, and the place list is a file that ships with the page. Nothing you type is uploaded, and nothing is saved unless you copy the link or download the PDF yourself.

**Can I make a kundli for a birth outside India?**
Yes. The place list covers towns worldwide, and the tool applies the time zone that was in force on that date, including summer time, so a birth in London or New Jersey is handled the same way as one in Jaipur.

**Why does my kundli look different on another website?**
Usually the ayanamsa or the house system. This tool uses the Lahiri ayanamsa, the mean lunar node for Rahu and Ketu, and whole-sign houses, where the lagna's sign is the whole first house. A site using a different ayanamsa or a cusp-based house system will shift some placements.

**Is astrology science?**
No. The astronomy here is real — the planetary positions are computed from modern ephemeris models — but the meanings that astrology attaches to them are a tradition, not a scientific finding. Use the kundli for cultural and personal interest, not for medical, legal or financial decisions.

#### Frequently asked questions (17)

**How is attendance percentage calculated?**
Divide the classes you attended by the total classes held, then multiply by 100. If you attended 45 of 60 classes that is 45 ÷ 60 × 100 = 75%. Nothing else — extra credit, medical leave and duty leave are institutional adjustments to the counts, not to the formula, so enter the counts your college actually records.

**How many classes can I bunk and still keep 75%?**
It depends on how far above the line you already are, not on a fixed number. If you have attended 90 of 100 classes you can miss 20 more before dropping to 75%. If you have attended 76 of 100 you can miss exactly one. The calculator works this out from your own numbers rather than quoting the common but wrong rule of thumb that you can always skip a quarter of the term.

**Why do I have to attend so many classes to gain one percent?**
Because missing a class and attending one are not opposites. Missing a class adds one to the total held only. Attending one adds to both the attended count and the total, so it moves the ratio much more slowly. That asymmetry is why recovering from 70% takes far more classes than dropping from 80% did — and why this tool reports the two numbers separately.

**Is 75% attendance a legal requirement in India?**
75% is the figure most Indian universities quote and the one commonly linked to UGC guidance, but the rule is set by each institution, not by a single national law. Plenty of colleges use 70% or 80%, some count practicals separately from lectures, and many allow relaxation on medical or duty grounds. Set the requirement field to whatever your own handbook states rather than assuming 75.

**Should I calculate attendance per subject or overall?**
Check both, because most colleges enforce a minimum per subject as well as overall. Use the Subject-wise tab to see each one. Note that the overall figure is the ratio of total attended to total held — not the average of your subject percentages. Averaging treats a ten-class lab and a forty-lecture course as equals and can tell you that you are fine when you are not.

**What if I can no longer reach the requirement?**
Enter the number of classes left in the term and the calculator will tell you the highest percentage you can still finish on. If that number is below your requirement it says so plainly instead of showing a target you cannot hit. At that point the useful next step is your department office — condonation, medical leave and duty leave are decided there, not by arithmetic.

**Does this tool store my attendance data?**
Your subject list is saved in your own browser's local storage so it is still there when you come back. It never leaves your device and we cannot see it. Clearing your browser data removes it, and the Reset button clears it immediately.

**Do medical leave and duty leave count as attendance?**
That is an institutional decision, not a mathematical one. Some colleges add approved leave to your attended count, some exclude those classes from the total held, and some do neither until you apply for condonation. Whichever your college does, enter the resulting numbers here — the arithmetic is the same once the counts are settled.

**What counts as a class — a lecture, a lab or a tutorial?**
Whatever your college counts. Many institutions weight a three-hour lab as three periods, which means one missed lab can cost the same as three missed lectures. If your college does that, enter periods rather than sessions so the percentage matches the one on your portal.

**What happens if my attendance is short?**
It depends how short. Within the band your college allows — often 65% to 75% — you can usually apply for condonation on medical or duty grounds, generally with a fee and supporting documents. Below that band, most institutions detain you from the examination for that subject, which means repeating it with the next batch. Your department office decides both, not the arithmetic.

**What is condonation of attendance shortage?**
Formal permission to sit the exam despite being below the requirement. It is normally available only within a band above a hard floor, needs a written application with evidence, and often carries a fee. Treat it as a discretion your department exercises, not a right you can plan around.

**How do I calculate attendance for six subjects?**
Use the Subject-wise tab and add a row per subject. Check each one against your requirement, because most colleges enforce a per-subject minimum as well as an overall one. The overall figure shown is the ratio of your total attended to your total held — not the average of the six percentages, which would misreport a small lab against a large lecture course.

**Is attendance counted per semester or for the whole year?**
Almost always per semester, and reset at the start of the next one. A few programmes aggregate across the year. Enter whichever period your portal reports, since the calculation is the same either way.

**Does low attendance affect my marks or only exam eligibility?**
Usually eligibility rather than marks — you are either permitted to sit the paper or you are not. Some institutions do award internal or sessional marks partly on attendance, in which case a shortage costs you twice. Check your scheme of examination.

**How many classes can I miss if 60 have been held?**
With perfect attendance so far and a 75% rule, twenty. The reference table above lists the figure for common totals at 65%, 70%, 75% and 80%. If you have already missed some, enter your own numbers - the budget depends on where you actually are, not on the total alone.

**Can I work out attendance for a date range?**
No — this tool works on counts, not dates. Take the attended and held figures for whatever period you care about from your portal and enter those. A date-based calculation would need your timetable and the college calendar, including every holiday and cancelled class, which no calculator can know.

**Can I use this for school rather than college?**
Yes. The arithmetic is identical — only the requirement changes. Schools commonly set 75% or 80% for board exam eligibility. Set the requirement to your school's figure and the rest works the same.

#### Frequently asked questions (17)

**What is four-line ruling and why does it matter?**
Four-line ruling is the standard paper for teaching English handwriting: a top line, a dashed middle line, a solid writing line and a bottom line. Small letters like a, c and e sit between the dashed line and the writing line. Tall letters like h, l and b reach the top line. Letters like g, p and y drop below the writing line to the bottom one. Those relative heights are most of what handwriting practice is teaching, which is why a sheet with letters that do not match its own lines is worse than no sheet at all.

**Do the letters actually line up with the ruling?**
Yes, by construction. The lines are calculated from the font's own proportions, so an ascender ends exactly on the top line at every line height - rather than the usual approach of fixing the lines and scaling the font to roughly fit.

**Which tracing style should I choose?**
Faded grey letters suit the youngest children, because the whole letter shape is visible and the pencil simply covers it. Dotted is the classic school style and gives a clearer path to follow. Outline suits children who already form letters and are working on consistency, since only the edges are given. All three print with no watermark.

**Can I make a name tracing worksheet?**
Yes — that is the most common use, and there is a one-tap Name practice sheet for it. Tap it, then replace the sample name with the child's own. It arrives at 22mm rows with a dotted outline and a model row on top, which is what a four-year-old needs; change the size or style afterwards if you want something different.

**Does it support Hindi or other Indian languages?**
Yes. There is a Devanagari ruling that gives the letters a headline to hang from rather than a baseline to sit on, which is how the script is actually written and taught — choosing the Hindi vowel preset switches to it automatically. You can type any Devanagari text. One honest limit remains: the five handwriting faces here are Latin fonts, so Devanagari falls back to your system font. It prints correctly and lines up with the ruling, but it will not look handwritten the way the English letters do.

**How do I get a PDF instead of printing?**
Press Print and choose "Save as PDF" as the destination — every current browser offers it. That route produces vector text, so the letters stay crisp at any zoom. Set the paper size in the print dialog to match the one you chose here.

**Why does the printed sheet look different from the screen?**
Almost always because the browser is scaling to fit. In the print dialog set the scale to 100% (not "Fit to page") and turn off headers and footers. The sheet is laid out in real millimetres, so at 100% the line height you chose is the line height that prints.

**What line height should I use?**
As a rough guide: 18 to 22 mm for nursery and kindergarten, 12 to 16 mm for early primary, and 8 to 11 mm once handwriting is established. Start larger than you think — a child who cannot fit a letter in the space learns to write cramped.

**Is anything I type uploaded?**
No. The sheet is built in your browser and printed by your browser. The only thing fetched from outside is the handwriting font file itself, from Google Fonts. Nothing you type — including a child's name — is ever sent anywhere.

**What order should a child learn letters in?**
By family rather than A to Z. Letters that start with the same pencil movement are taught together - the anticlockwise curve of c carries most of a, d, g, o and q - so a child is not switching movement on every letter. The tables above list the families and load them into the sheet in one tap.

**Should we start with capitals or small letters?**
Capitals, and the straight-line ones first — E, F, H, I, L, T. They are the most forgiving shapes, because a slightly wobbly straight line still reads as the right letter, so a child gets an early win before tackling curves.

**Can this make cursive or joined-up writing sheets?**
Not properly. The fonts here are print handwriting faces, so letters are formed correctly but not joined. A true cursive sheet needs a font with connecting entry and exit strokes; using a script font without them produces letters that look joined in the preview and are not, which teaches the wrong join.

**Any advice for a left-handed child?**
Tilt the paper clockwise rather than anticlockwise so the hand sits below the line instead of dragging through it, and hold the pencil slightly further from the tip so the hand does not cover what was just written. On the sheet, pick the model row then trace layout — a model on the row above stays visible, one on the same line does not.

**How many practice sheets a day is reasonable?**
Short and often beats long and occasional. Five to ten minutes daily does more than a full page once a week, and a page of the same letter past the point of concentration mostly practises the mistakes. That is what rows per item is for — two or three good rows, not twenty.

**Why do the letters look different from my child's school book?**
Schools pick a house style and stick to it, and they differ — some teach a looped f, some a straight one; some teach an entry stroke on small letters, some do not. Five fonts are offered here so you can pick the closest match. If none of them matches, follow the school's book for shapes and use these sheets for size and spacing practice.

**Can I use these sheets for an adult improving their handwriting?**
Yes. Drop the line height to 8-10 mm, choose the hollow outline so only the shape is given, and use the sentence wrap so the practice is about spacing and consistency rather than letter formation, which an adult already knows.

**How is this different from the Text to Handwriting tool?**
Opposite jobs. That one turns typed text into something that looks handwritten. This one makes a blank practice sheet for someone to write on.

Free, with no sign-up and no watermark. There is no limit on the number of rooms, and each room can be up to 60 rows by 60 columns. A single class range is capped at 20,000 roll numbers, which is well past what any hall arrangement needs and exists only so a mistyped range cannot freeze the page.

#### Frequently asked questions (18)

**Are the generated bingo cards really unique?**
Yes. Every card is compared against the ones already produced and is discarded if it matches, so a batch of 30 contains 30 genuinely different cards. If your settings cannot produce that many distinct layouts, the tool tells you how many it managed rather than padding the batch with duplicates.

**How many bingo cards can I make at once?**
Up to 500 in one batch. They are laid out at 1, 2 or 4 cards per sheet, so 500 cards is 125 sheets at four per page.

**What is the difference between 75-ball and 90-ball bingo?**
75-ball is the American game: a 5 by 5 grid with a free centre square and columns headed B, I, N, G and O drawing from 1-15, 16-30, 31-45, 46-60 and 61-75. 90-ball is the British game: a 3 by 9 grid holding exactly 15 numbers, five per row, with no free square.

**Can I make bingo cards with my own words?**
Yes. Switch to Words mode and paste your list with one item per line. A 5 by 5 card with a free square needs at least 24 items, and the more items you supply beyond that, the more different cards become possible.

**How many words do I need for bingo cards?**
At least as many as there are squares — 24 for a 5 by 5 card with a free centre, or 25 without. For a class of 30 children you want noticeably more than the minimum, otherwise the cards will share most of their squares. The tool shows how many distinct cards your list can produce.

**What does the seed do?**
The seed drives the shuffling, so the same seed always produces exactly the same batch of cards. That means you can reprint a lost card, or verify a card someone brings back weeks later, without keeping the file.

**Can I verify a winning card?**
Yes. Enter the card ID or the seed printed on the card, paste the calls that have been made, and pick the pattern. The checker matches each call exactly against each square, and if it is not a win it tells you how close the card is and which squares are still missing.

**Why does the win checker reject a card that looks like a winner?**
Most often because a call was mistyped or missed from the list. The checker deliberately requires an exact match, so B7 does not satisfy a square holding B75 — loose matching would confirm wins that never happened.

**Which win patterns are supported?**
Any line, any row, any column, any diagonal, four corners, an X, the outer frame, and a full blackout. Diagonals and the X are only offered on square grids, because a 3 by 9 card has no diagonal.

**Does the digital caller repeat numbers?**
No. It draws from the remaining calls only, so nothing comes up twice, and it stops when every call has been drawn. You can undo the last call or reset the whole game.

**Can the caller read the numbers out loud?**
Yes, using your browser's built-in speech. Turn on spoken calls and each draw is announced. The available voices come from your operating system, so quality varies between devices.

**How do I print bingo cards at home?**
Choose your paper size and how many cards go on each sheet, then use Print or download the PDF. Turn on cut lines to see where to cut, and make sure your print dialog is set to actual size rather than fit to page.

**What is ink-saver mode?**
It drops the coloured fills and prints black outlines on white, which uses far less ink on a large batch and stays readable on a monochrome laser printer.

**Can I download the cards as images instead of a PDF?**
Yes. The ZIP download gives you one PNG per card, named by card ID, which suits sending cards to players individually or dropping them into a slide deck.

**Does this work offline?**

No watermark, no sign-up and no limit on how many batches you make. The only cap is 500 cards in a single batch, which keeps the browser responsive.
