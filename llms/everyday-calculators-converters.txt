#### Frequently asked questions (28)

**What is a percentage?**
A percentage is a fraction expressed out of 100. The word comes from the Latin per centum, meaning by the hundred. Writing 25% is another way of writing 25/100, or the decimal 0.25 — three notations for exactly the same quantity.

**How do I calculate a percentage?**
It depends which of two questions you are asking. To find a percentage of a number, divide the percentage by 100 and multiply. To find what percentage one number is of another, divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. Confusing the two is the most common percentage mistake there is.

**What is the percentage formula?**
Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100. Rearranged, Part = (Percentage ÷ 100) × Whole, and Whole = Part ÷ (Percentage ÷ 100). Those three arrangements of one relationship answer most percentage questions.

**How do I find 10% of a number?**
Move the decimal point one place to the left: 10% of 500 is 50, and 10% of 87 is 8.7. From there, 5% is half of that, 20% is double it, and 15% is 10% plus 5% — which makes most everyday percentages workable in your head.

**How do I calculate percentage increase?**
Subtract the original value from the new one, divide by the original, then multiply by 100. From 100 to 125 is (125 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 25%. Dividing by the original rather than the new value is what makes it an increase from that starting point.

**How do I calculate percentage decrease?**
The same formula. From 100 to 75 is (75 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = −25%, a 25% decrease. Increase and decrease are the same calculation; only the sign differs.

**How do I calculate percentage change?**
((New − Original) ÷ Original) × 100. A positive result is an increase and a negative one is a decrease. Where the original value is negative this calculator divides by its magnitude, so a move from −100 to −50 correctly reads as an improvement.

**What is the difference between percentage change and percentage difference?**
Percentage change measures a move from a specific starting point and has a direction, so it is not symmetric: 100 to 120 is a 20% increase, but 120 to 100 is a 16.67% decrease. Percentage difference compares two values against their average, has no direction and is symmetric — 100 and 120 differ by 18.18% whichever order you take them in.

**How do I find what percent one number is of another?**
Divide the first number by the second and multiply by 100. 45 out of 60 is 45 ÷ 60 × 100 = 75%. The number you are measuring goes on top; the number you are measuring against goes on the bottom.

**How do I reverse a percentage?**
If you know a part and what percentage it represents, divide the part by the percentage as a decimal. 50 being 25% of something means the whole is 50 ÷ 0.25 = 200. Dividing, not multiplying, is what makes it a reversal.

**How do I calculate a discount?**
Multiply the price by the discount percentage and divide by 100 to get the saving, then subtract it. Or in one step, multiply the price by (1 − discount ÷ 100). A 20% discount on ₹2,000 saves ₹400 and leaves ₹1,600.

**How do I find the original price after a discount?**
Divide the price you paid by (1 − discount ÷ 100). ₹800 after a 20% discount was ₹1,000 originally, because ₹800 ÷ 0.8 = ₹1,000. Adding 20% back to ₹800 gives ₹960, which is wrong — that is the single most common reverse-percentage error.

**How do I calculate markup?**
Markup is measured against cost. Multiply the cost by the markup percentage and divide by 100, then add it. A 25% markup on a ₹1,000 cost gives a ₹250 markup and a ₹1,250 selling price.

**What is the difference between markup and margin?**
Both describe the same profit, measured against different bases. Markup divides profit by cost; margin divides the same profit by the selling price. A 25% markup is a 20% margin, and because the selling price is always the larger denominator, margin is always the smaller number.

**Can a percentage be greater than 100%?**
Yes, and this calculator does not block it. 150% of 200 is 300, and a value that triples has increased by 200%. A percentage above 100 simply means the part is larger than the whole it is being measured against.

**Can percentages be negative?**
Yes. A negative percentage change is a decrease, and negative values are handled consistently rather than silently converted to positive. Where a negative starting value would make the sign misleading, the calculator says so.

**How do I calculate a percentage with decimals?**
Exactly as you would a whole percentage — 12.5% of 800 is 0.125 × 800 = 100. This calculator accepts decimal percentages and decimal values, and lets you control how many decimal places the answer is displayed to.

**Why does my calculator show 33.33333333?**
Because one third has no exact decimal representation. This calculator rounds for display while keeping full precision internally, and removes the floating-point artifacts that otherwise turn 70 into 70.00000000000001.

**What is a percentage point?**
The arithmetic difference between two percentages. Going from 20% to 25% is a rise of 5 percentage points, but a 25% increase in relative terms. Reporting one as the other is a common and sometimes deliberate distortion.

**Why does a 20% loss need more than a 20% gain to recover?**
Because the second percentage applies to a smaller base. Losing 20% of 100 leaves 80, and getting back to 100 from 80 requires a 25% gain. Percentage changes do not cancel out, which is why sequential percentages have to be applied in order rather than added together.

**How do I apply two percentages one after another?**
Multiply the factors rather than adding the percentages. A 20% discount followed by a further 10% is 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72, a 28% total discount — not 30%. Two successive 50% discounts do not make an item free.

**How do I calculate 20% of 500?**
20 ÷ 100 × 500 = 100. Or find 10% by moving the decimal point one place left, which gives 50, and double it.

**How do I calculate 15% of 1,000?**
15 ÷ 100 × 1,000 = 150. Mentally: 10% is 100 and 5% is half of that at 50, so 15% is 150.

**Can I use this calculator for discounts and prices?**
Yes. There are dedicated modes for discount, reverse discount, markup, margin, adding tax and removing tax, and those modes format their results as currency in rupees, dollars, euros or pounds.

**How do I remove tax from a tax-inclusive price?**
Divide the total by (1 + tax rate ÷ 100). ₹1,180 including 18% tax contains ₹1,000 before tax and ₹180 of tax. Taking 18% of ₹1,180 gives ₹212.40, which overstates the tax because that figure already contains it.

**Does it support sin, cos and tan?**
Yes, along with their inverses (asin, acos, atan), the hyperbolic versions (sinh, cosh, tanh) and the inverse hyperbolics. All of them respect the DEG, RAD or GRAD setting you have selected.

**What is DEG mode?**
Degrees, where a full circle is 360. It is what school geometry, surveying and most engineering drawings use. In DEG mode sin(30) equals 0.5.

**What is RAD mode?**
Radians, where a full circle is 2π ≈ 6.283. It is the natural unit for calculus and physics, because the derivative of sin(x) is cos(x) only when x is in radians. In RAD mode sin(pi/2) equals 1.

**What is GRAD mode?**
Gradians, where a full circle is 400 and a right angle is exactly 100. It appears mainly in some European surveying and civil engineering traditions. In GRAD mode sin(100) equals 1.

**How do I calculate a factorial?**
Type the number followed by an exclamation mark: 6! gives 720. Factorials are defined for non-negative whole numbers, and 0! is 1 by definition. They grow extremely fast - 20! is already about 2.4 × 10¹⁸.

**How do I calculate square roots?**
Use sqrt(144) for 12, cbrt(27) for cube roots, or nthRoot(32, 5) for any other root. A root is just a fractional power, so x^(1/2) gives the same answer as sqrt(x).

**How do I calculate powers?**
Use the caret: 2^10 gives 1024. Note two conventions - exponents bind tighter than a leading minus, so -2^2 is −4 and you need (-2)^2 for 4; and exponentiation is right-associative, so 2^3^2 is 2^9 = 512 rather than 64.

**What is the difference between log and ln?**
In this engine log(x) is the natural logarithm and log10(x) is base 10, which is the programming-library convention and the opposite of the key labels on a physical Casio or TI. If a logarithm looks wrong by a factor of about 2.303 - which is ln(10) - this is why. For any other base, log(x, b) works directly.

**What is scientific notation?**
Writing a number as a value between 1 and 10 times a power of ten, such as 6.022 × 10²³. Type it as 6.022e23. The display switches to it automatically above 10¹⁵ and below 10⁻⁹, where the plain digit string stops being readable.

**Can I use keyboard shortcuts?**
Yes, and it is the fastest way to use it. Type the whole expression as text; Enter evaluates, Escape clears, Backspace deletes, and the arrow keys, Home and End move the cursor. Because the input is an ordinary text field, select, copy and paste all work as usual.

**Does it keep a calculation history?**
Yes - a running tape. Every calculation is recorded with its expression and result. Click a past result to insert it into the current expression, or edit a past line and everything after it recalculates in order. That last part is what physical calculators cannot do.

**Does it have memory functions?**
Yes, plus named variables. Memory is the classic store-and-recall behaviour for a running subtotal. Variables let you name a value - assign r = 4.2 and then write pi * r^2 - which is far less error-prone for multi-step work and makes the tape readable afterwards.

**Does it work offline?**
Yes, after your first visit. A service worker caches the page and the maths engine, so it opens and calculates with no connection - including your tape, memory and variables, which live on your device.

**Are calculations sent to a server?**
No. Expressions are parsed and evaluated entirely in your browser; there is no server component to send them to. Open developer tools, go to the Network tab and run some calculations - you will see the page's files and the maths engine load once and nothing afterwards.

**Does it use eval()?**
No. Expressions are tokenised and evaluated by a dedicated maths library with its own parser. That matters for accuracy - it applies real operator precedence - and for security, since nothing you type into the field can execute as code.

**Why does 0.1 + 0.2 not give exactly 0.3 on some calculators?**
Because computers store numbers in binary and some decimal fractions have no exact binary form, the same way 1/3 has no exact decimal form. The raw result is 0.30000000000000004. This calculator rounds at the display layer, so you see 0.3, while the underlying value keeps full precision so errors do not accumulate through a chain of calculations.

**How precise is it?**
It uses standard double-precision floating point, giving about 15 to 17 significant decimal digits. That is comfortably enough for engineering, physics and school mathematics. It is not arbitrary precision - if you need fifty digits of π, this is the wrong instrument.

**Why does my trig answer look wrong?**
Almost always the angle mode. sin(30) is 0.5 in DEG but about −0.988 in RAD, because 30 radians is a completely different angle. Neither is an error; only one answers the question you meant. Check the DEG/RAD/GRAD indicator at the top left before trusting any trigonometric result.

#### Frequently asked questions (30)

**What is BMI (Body Mass Index)?**
BMI is a simple screening measurement that estimates body fatness from your weight and height, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. It's used worldwide by organizations like the WHO and CDC as a quick population-level indicator, not a diagnostic tool.

**What is the BMI formula?**
The metric formula is BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². The imperial formula is BMI = 703 × weight (lb) ÷ height (in)². Both formulas produce the same BMI value for the same body — only the unit conversion constant differs.

**How do I calculate BMI in metric units?**
Divide your weight in kilograms by your height in metres, then divide again by the same height in metres. For example, 70 kg ÷ (1.70 m × 1.70 m) = 24.2.

**How do I calculate BMI in imperial units?**
Multiply your weight in pounds by 703, then divide by your height in inches squared. For example, a 154 lb person at 67 inches: (703 × 154) ÷ (67 × 67) ≈ 24.1.

**What are the BMI categories?**
WHO defines Underweight as below 18.5, Normal weight as 18.5–24.9, Overweight as 25–29.9, and Obesity as 30 and above, further split into Class I (30–34.9), Class II (35–39.9), and Class III (40+).

**What BMI is considered healthy?**
A BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is classified as the healthy/normal range by WHO for most adults, though the healthy range narrows to roughly 18.5–22.9 under the WHO Asia-Pacific cutoffs sometimes recommended for South Asian populations.

**What is a healthy weight range for my height?**
Your healthy weight range is the weight span that keeps your BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 (or 18.5–22.9 under Asian cutoffs) at your current height. This calculator computes both ends of that range automatically and shows where your current weight sits on the scale.

**What is my ideal weight, and how is it calculated here?**
This tool averages four validated formulas — Devine, Robinson, Miller, and Hamwi — which estimate ideal body weight from height and sex. Averaging multiple formulas gives a more balanced estimate than relying on any single one.

**Is BMI different for men and women?**
The BMI formula and category thresholds are the same for men and women. However, women naturally carry more essential body fat than men at the same BMI, which is why this calculator's body fat, ideal weight, and BMR estimates use separate validated formulas for each sex.

**Is BMI accurate for older adults?**
BMI tends to under-represent body fat in older adults because muscle mass typically declines with age while fat mass may stay stable or increase, sometimes called sarcopenic obesity. Waist circumference and body composition measures are useful complements for adults over 65.

**Is BMI accurate for athletes and muscular people?**
No, not reliably. Muscle is denser than fat, so athletes and heavily muscled individuals can have a BMI in the overweight or obese range while carrying very low body fat. Enable Athlete Mode in Advanced Options to see a caveat, and rely more on the waist-based and body fat estimates in that case.

**Can I use BMI during pregnancy?**
Standard BMI categories don't apply during pregnancy because healthy weight gain is expected and varies by trimester and pre-pregnancy weight. Enable Pregnancy Mode to disable category interpretation — always follow your obstetrician's specific gestational weight gain guidance instead.

**What are the limitations of BMI?**
BMI doesn't distinguish muscle from fat, doesn't account for fat distribution, and can misclassify athletes, older adults, and some ethnic groups. It's a useful population-level screening tool, not an individual diagnostic measure — pair it with waist measurements and a clinical assessment where possible.

**What is waist-to-height ratio and why does it matter?**
Waist-to-height ratio (waist ÷ height) is a simple proxy for abdominal fat, which is more strongly linked to cardiometabolic risk than total body weight. A widely cited rule of thumb is to keep your waist circumference below half your height.

**What is waist-to-hip ratio?**
Waist-to-hip ratio (waist ÷ hip) indicates whether fat is stored more around the abdomen (apple shape) or hips/thighs (pear shape). WHO considers a ratio above 0.90 in men or 0.85 in women to indicate increased health risk.

**Does BMI measure body fat?**
No. BMI only uses weight and height — it cannot distinguish fat mass from muscle, bone, or water weight. It correlates with body fat at a population level but can be misleading for individuals with atypical body composition.

**How is body fat percentage estimated on this page?**
This tool uses the U.S. Navy circumference method, which estimates body fat from waist, neck, and height (plus hip for women) using validated logarithmic formulas. It's a reasonably accurate tape-measure estimate, though not as precise as a DEXA scan or hydrostatic weighing.

**What is BMR and how is it calculated?**
Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is the energy your body needs at complete rest to maintain basic functions. This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, widely regarded as the most accurate predictive formula for BMR, based on your weight, height, age, and sex.

**What is TDEE and how is it different from BMR?**
Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) is your BMR multiplied by an activity factor that accounts for exercise and daily movement. BMR alone reflects resting energy needs; TDEE estimates the calories you actually burn in a full day, which is the number to use for weight management.

**How many calories should I eat to lose weight?**
A deficit of about 500 kcal/day below your TDEE typically produces roughly 0.5 kg (about 1 lb) of weight loss per week, since roughly 7,700 kcal corresponds to 1 kg of body weight. Always avoid extreme deficits without medical supervision.

**How many calories should I eat to gain weight?**
A surplus of roughly 250–500 kcal/day above your TDEE supports gradual, mostly lean weight gain of about 0.25–0.5 kg per week; larger surpluses tend to add more fat relative to muscle.

**Is BMI different for Indian or South Asian populations?**
Research shows South Asians tend to develop cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI values than the global WHO cutoffs suggest, largely due to a tendency toward higher body fat percentage at the same BMI. The WHO Asia-Pacific guidelines lower the overweight cutoff to 23 and obesity to 27.5 — this calculator lets you switch to those cutoffs in Advanced Options.

**What do the WHO and CDC say about BMI?**
Both the World Health Organization and the U.S. CDC use the same core adult BMI categories (Underweight, Normal, Overweight, Obese) as a population-level screening tool, while explicitly noting that BMI does not directly measure body fat and should be interpreted alongside other health indicators.

**What are common BMI calculation mistakes?**
Common errors include mixing units, using outdated height, forgetting that BMI categories don't apply to children (who use age- and sex-specific percentile charts) or pregnant women, and treating a single BMI reading as a medical diagnosis rather than a screening signal.

#### Frequently asked questions (24)

**What is the difference between GPA, SGPA and CGPA?**
GPA (Grade Point Average) measures performance over a set of courses. SGPA (Semester GPA) is the GPA for a single semester. CGPA (Cumulative GPA) is the credit-weighted average of all your SGPAs across every semester completed so far.

**How do you calculate GPA from grades and credit hours?**
Multiply each course's grade point by its credit hours, add up those totals, then divide by the total credit hours. For example, an A (4.0) in a 3-credit course contributes 12 grade points toward the average.

**How is CGPA calculated from multiple semesters?**
Multiply each semester's SGPA by that semester's total credits, add the results together, then divide by the combined credit hours across all semesters. This weights semesters with more credits more heavily, the same way individual courses are weighted within a semester.

**How do I convert CGPA to percentage?**
There is no single official formula - it depends on your university. Common approaches multiply CGPA by 9.5 or 9.4, or use the offset formula Percentage = (CGPA − 0.75) × 10 used by some Indian universities on a 10-point scale. Always confirm the exact formula with your institution for official purposes.

**What is a good GPA or CGPA?**
On a 4.0 scale, 3.5 or higher is generally considered excellent, 3.0-3.49 is good, and 2.0-2.99 is average. On a 10-point scale, 8.5 or higher is typically considered excellent and 7-8.5 is good, though classifications vary by university.

**How do I find out what GPA I need to reach a target?**
Enter your current GPA, current credit hours, target GPA, and remaining credit hours into the target calculator. It works out the average grade you need across the remaining credits, and flags it if that average would exceed the maximum possible on your scale.

Multiply kilograms by 2.20462. One pound is exactly 0.45359237 kilograms, which is an exact definition rather than a rounded figure, so 70 kg is 154.32 lb.

**How do I convert Celsius to Fahrenheit?**
Multiply by 9/5 and add 32, so 20 °C is 68 °F. Temperature is not a simple ratio because the scales have different zero points, which is why a converter that only multiplies gets it wrong. A useful check: −40 °C and −40 °F are the same temperature.

**Why is a US gallon different from a UK gallon?**
They are simply different units that share a name. A US gallon is 3.785411784 litres and an imperial gallon is 4.54609 litres, so the UK gallon is about 20 percent larger. Both are listed separately here so you never have to guess which one is meant.

**What is the difference between a US ton and a UK ton?**
A US short ton is 2,000 pounds and a UK long ton is 2,240 pounds, while a metric ton is 1,000 kilograms. All three are listed separately, because picking the wrong one is a 10 percent error.

**Why does my 1 TB hard drive show as 931 GB?**
Because two different definitions are in use. Drive makers sell a terabyte as one trillion bytes, while operating systems usually count in binary units of 1,024. One trillion bytes is 931.32 gibibytes, so nothing is missing — the two systems are just counting differently. This tool lists decimal and binary units separately so you can see both.

**How do I convert Mbps to MB/s?**
Divide by eight, because there are eight bits in a byte. A 100 Mbps connection downloads at about 12.5 MB/s at best. Internet plans are advertised in megabits while download managers usually report megabytes, which is why the numbers look so different.

**How do I convert mpg to L/100km?**
Divide 235.21 by the US mpg figure, so 30 mpg is about 7.84 L/100km. Fuel economy is inverse: a higher mpg means a lower L/100km, so you cannot convert it by multiplying. The tool handles that inversion for you.

**Is a US mpg the same as a UK mpg?**
No. They use different gallons, so the same car scores about 20 percent higher in UK mpg than in US mpg. Both are listed separately in the fuel economy category.

1 अंकनम = 72 वर्ग फुट = 8 वर्ग गज। यह आंध्र प्रदेश और तेलंगाना में, खासकर घर बनाने वाले छोटे प्लॉट के लिए इस्तेमाल होती है।

**लंबाई और चौड़ाई से ज़मीन का क्षेत्रफल कैसे निकालें?**
चौकोर या आयताकार प्लॉट के लिए लंबाई × चौड़ाई करें। जैसे 60 फुट × 40 फुट का प्लॉट 2,400 वर्ग फुट का होता है, यानी लगभग 266.67 गज। इस पेज के कनवर्टर टैब में दिया प्लॉट कैलकुलेटर यही गणना करके उसे बीघा, कट्ठा या गुंठा में भी बदल देता है।

**क्या मैं अपना रूपांतरण किसी को भेज सकता हूँ?**
हाँ। 'लिंक कॉपी करें' बटन दबाने पर एक ऐसा लिंक बन जाता है जिसमें आपका राज्य, मान और चुनी हुई इकाइयाँ शामिल होती हैं — सामने वाला उसे खोलते ही वही नतीजा देखेगा।
