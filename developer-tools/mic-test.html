A level meter tells you the microphone is picking something up. That is not the question you arrived with — you want to know whether you sound right. So this tool does both: a live meter calibrated in decibels with a waveform, and a short recording played back so you hear exactly what the other side hears.

🔒 The audio stays on your device — no upload, no storage, no account.

A 30-Second Mic Check Before a Call

  1. Pick the microphone your call app will use. The list here and the app's own input setting have to name the same device.
  2. Talk at your normal volume. Aim for the verdict to read "Good level" — peaks between −35 and −6 dB.
  3. Press Record 6 seconds and say a sentence you would really say on the call.
  4. Play it back through headphones. Listen for hum, echo, or a muffled, distant sound — none of them show on a meter.
  5. Press Stop microphone when you are done, so the browser's recording indicator goes out.

The bar, the number and the marker

The coloured bar
Your average loudness (RMS) — the steady level of your voice.
The dB number
The loudest point in the latest slice of audio, updated many times a second. The verdict under the meter is judged from this peak.
The white marker
The highest recent peak, held for just under a second so short spikes are easy to catch.

Why the playback matters more than the meter

Most microphone testers show you a bar that wiggles. That answers exactly one question — is any signal arriving — and it is rarely the question people actually have. Hum from a laptop charger, echo from speakers, a muffled built-in microphone, a fan in the background, a headset picking up your breathing: none of that shows on a meter. All of it is obvious the second you hear the recording.

That is why this tool records six seconds and plays them back. It is the same reason sound engineers listen before they trust a level: you cannot see a problem you can only hear.

The live view is not decoration either. The meter is calibrated in decibels rather than being an arbitrary bar, so "too quiet" and "clipping" are measurements rather than guesses, and a peak marker holds the loudest moment for a beat so you can catch spikes the eye would otherwise miss.

Reading the meter

What each part of the range means when you are speaking normally
ReadingVerdictWhat to do
−60 dB Silent Nothing is arriving. Check the hardware mute switch and the selected device.
−55 to −35 dB Very quiet Move closer or raise the input level in your system's sound settings.
−35 to −6 dB Good Nothing. This is where you want to be.
−6 to −1 dB Loud Back off slightly so sudden louder words have somewhere to go.
−1 to 0 dB Clipping Lower the input level. Clipped audio is distorted permanently and cannot be repaired afterwards.

Decibels here are dBFS — decibels relative to the loudest value the system can represent, so 0 is the ceiling and everything else is negative. The scale is logarithmic, which is why the meter is drawn linear in decibels rather than in raw amplitude: an amplitude bar spends nearly all its travel in the top few decibels and looks broken at normal speaking volume.

Fixing a microphone that will not work

The meter does not move at all

Check the physical mute first. Most headsets have an inline mute switch or a mute button on the earcup, and many laptops have a mute key with its own indicator light — these override everything in software and nothing on screen will tell you they are engaged. Then check the device list here: laptops very often stay on the built-in microphone even after a headset is plugged in.

"The device is busy"

Another application is holding the microphone. Zoom, Teams, Meet, Discord, OBS and voice recorders all keep it open in the background, and minimising the window does not release it. Quit them properly and try again.

"Permission was refused"

Click the microphone or lock icon in the address bar, set microphone access to Allow, and reload. In Chrome and Edge the full list is under Settings → Privacy and security → Site settings → Microphone. On Windows, also check Settings → Privacy & security → Microphone; on a Mac, System Settings → Privacy & Security → Microphone.

It works here but not in one specific app

Then the microphone is fine and that app has its own input selected. Every call application keeps a device setting separate from the system default, and they routinely disagree. Open the audio settings inside that app and pick the same device you selected here.

There is a hum or buzz in the recording

A steady low hum is almost always electrical — an unearthed laptop charger is the usual culprit. Unplug the charger and record again. If the hum vanishes, try a different socket or a different charger, or move the microphone cable away from power cables.

You hear an echo of your own voice

Your speakers are being picked up by the microphone. Headphones fix it completely. The readouts show whether the browser's echo cancellation is on, which helps but cannot fully remove a loud speaker sitting next to an open microphone.

The three processing flags

Browsers apply audio processing before you ever hear the signal, and this tool reports whether each piece is active because it changes what you are measuring.

  • Echo cancellation subtracts your speakers' output from the microphone feed, so the far side does not hear themselves. It also slightly dulls your voice.
  • Noise suppression removes steady background sound such as a fan or air conditioning. It can make a quiet voice sound thin, because it cannot always tell you apart from the noise.
  • Auto gain control raises and lowers the level for you. It is why the meter sometimes settles into a comfortable range on its own — and why a genuinely quiet microphone can look fine on a meter while sounding noisy in playback, since the noise gets amplified along with your voice.

All three make calls better and measurements less literal. If a reading says "Not reported", the browser simply did not expose it — Firefox and Safari are less forthcoming here than Chrome, and that is not a fault.

Privacy — what happens to the audio

The microphone feeds an analyser inside this page. Nothing is transmitted, and there is no server involved in the test at any point.

  • The live signal is never stored. It is measured frame by frame to draw the meter and waveform, and then discarded.
  • The recording lives in this tab only. It is held in memory, replaced when you record again, and gone when you close the page.
  • Downloading is the only way it becomes a file, and that save goes straight to your own downloads folder.
  • The microphone is released when you leave. Closing the tab or pressing Stop ends the track and the recording indicator goes out.

Your browser's own recording indicator is the ground truth, not any promise this page makes. Our full privacy policy covers the rest of the site.

Frequently asked questions

Is my audio uploaded or stored?

No. The microphone feeds an audio analyser inside this page and nothing is sent to a server. The recording is held in your browser's memory, is replaced the moment you record again, and disappears when you close the tab. Downloading it is the only way it becomes a file, and that save goes straight to your own downloads folder.

Why can I not hear myself live?

Because routing a live microphone to your speakers causes an instant feedback howl unless you are wearing headphones. That is why the tool records a few seconds and plays it back instead — you get to hear yourself without the screech, and playback is a more honest test anyway.

The meter moves but people say they cannot hear me. Why?

The meter proves the microphone works. If a specific app still cannot hear you, that app is using a different input device or has its own permission turned off. Check the audio input setting inside Zoom, Teams, Meet or Discord — each keeps its own device selection, separate from the system default.

The meter does not move at all.

Check the hardware mute first: most headsets have an inline mute switch or a mute button on the earcup, and many laptops have a mute key with an indicator light. Then check that the right device is selected in the list — laptops frequently default to the built-in microphone even when a headset is plugged in.

How loud should the meter be?

Speaking normally should put the peaks around the middle to upper-middle of the bar, roughly between −20 and −6 dB. Constantly hitting the top means clipping, which distorts and cannot be repaired afterwards. Barely moving means people will strain to hear you and background noise will be amplified along with your voice.

I sound muffled or distant in the playback.

Usually the wrong microphone is selected — a laptop's built-in microphone sounds distant compared with a headset. It can also be a physically blocked microphone: a case over a phone's port, a hand over a laptop's microphone pinholes, or a boom arm pointing away from your mouth. Try each device in the list and compare the recordings.

What are echo cancellation, noise suppression and auto gain?

Three pieces of processing the browser applies before you ever hear the signal. Echo cancellation removes your speakers' output from the microphone feed. Noise suppression removes steady background sound like a fan. Auto gain quietly raises or lowers the level for you. They make calls better but make measurement less honest, which is why the tool reports whether each one is on.

Why does my Bluetooth headset sound bad when the microphone is on?

Bluetooth cannot send high-quality audio and receive microphone audio at the same time. The moment a device opens the microphone, the headset switches to a much lower-quality two-way mode and music quality collapses. It is a limitation of Bluetooth itself, not of your headset or this page.

The microphone list is empty or the names are blank.

Browsers hide device names until microphone permission has been granted at least once for the site — a privacy measure in the specification, not a fault. Press Start microphone, allow access, and the list fills in with real names.

Does this work on a phone?

Yes, on Android and iPhone. On iOS, microphone access only works in Safari and in browsers built on the Safari engine. On both, playback comes out of the phone's speaker unless headphones are connected.

Do I need to install anything, or sign up?

No. There is no download, no extension and no account. The test uses the microphone and audio support built into your browser — the same interface video-call sites use.

Can I test my camera here too?

The camera has its own tool so that a microphone test never asks for camera permission you did not want to give. The Webcam Test shows a live preview along with the resolution and frame rate your camera actually delivers.

Same idea, other half of the call: a live preview plus the resolution and frame rate your camera is really sending, which is usually not what you were promised.
